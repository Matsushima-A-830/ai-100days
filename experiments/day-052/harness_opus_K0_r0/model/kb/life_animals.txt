# Animals: classification, adaptations, behavior, life cycles
Animals are consumers because they cannot make their own food and must eat plants or other animals.
Vertebrates are animals with a backbone; invertebrates are animals without a backbone.
Most animal species on Earth are invertebrates, especially insects.
The five groups of vertebrates are fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.
Mammals have hair or fur, breathe with lungs, are warm-blooded, and feed their young milk.
Most mammals give birth to live young; the platypus and echidna are mammals that lay eggs.
Whales, dolphins and bats are mammals.
Whales and dolphins are mammals that breathe air with lungs and must come to the surface to breathe.
Bats are the only mammals that can truly fly.
Birds have feathers, wings, beaks, hollow bones, are warm-blooded and lay eggs with hard shells.
Hollow bones make birds light for flying.
Penguins and ostriches are birds that cannot fly.
Reptiles have dry scaly skin, breathe with lungs, are cold-blooded and lay eggs with leathery shells on land.
Snakes, lizards, turtles, alligators and crocodiles are reptiles.
Amphibians such as frogs, toads and salamanders live part of their lives in water and part on land.
Amphibians have moist skin, lay jelly-like eggs in water, and young breathe with gills.
Fish live in water, breathe with gills, have fins and scales, and are cold-blooded.
Gills take dissolved oxygen out of water.
Sharks are fish with skeletons made of cartilage.
Cold-blooded animals, or ectotherms, have body temperatures that change with their surroundings.
Reptiles bask in the sun to warm their bodies because they are cold-blooded.
Warm-blooded animals, or endotherms, keep a constant body temperature.
Insects have six legs, three body parts (head, thorax and abdomen), antennae, and usually wings.
Spiders are arachnids with eight legs and two body parts; they are not insects.
Arthropods have jointed legs, segmented bodies and an exoskeleton.
Insects, spiders, crabs and lobsters are arthropods.
An exoskeleton is a hard outer covering that supports and protects the body; arthropods molt it as they grow.
Worms, jellyfish, sponges, snails, clams, octopuses and starfish are invertebrates.
Mollusks such as snails, clams and octopuses have soft bodies, often protected by a shell.
Earthworms breathe through their moist skin.
Metamorphosis is a major change in body form during the life cycle of an animal.
Complete metamorphosis has four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult.
Butterflies, moths, beetles, flies and bees go through complete metamorphosis.
A caterpillar is the larva of a butterfly; it eats leaves and grows.
In the pupa stage, a caterpillar forms a chrysalis and changes into an adult butterfly.
The life cycle of a butterfly is egg, larva or caterpillar, pupa or chrysalis, adult.
Incomplete metamorphosis has three stages: egg, nymph and adult.
Grasshoppers, crickets and dragonflies go through incomplete metamorphosis; a nymph looks like a small adult without wings.
The life cycle of a frog is egg, tadpole, young frog and adult frog.
A tadpole lives in water, breathes with gills and has a tail; an adult frog has lungs and legs.
Frogs go through metamorphosis from tadpole to adult.
Mammals grow and develop without metamorphosis; the young look like small adults.
Life spans differ: some insects live days, while some tortoises and whales live over 100 years.
An adaptation is a body part or behavior that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.
Structural adaptations are body parts, such as thick fur, sharp claws, long necks, webbed feet or beaks.
Behavioral adaptations are things animals do, such as migration, hibernation, and living in groups.
Camouflage is an adaptation that lets an animal blend in with its surroundings to hide from predators or sneak up on prey.
Some animals such as chameleons and octopuses can change color to blend in with their surroundings and hide.
A white coat of fur helps arctic animals such as polar bears and arctic foxes blend in with snow.
The arctic fox and snowshoe hare grow white fur in winter and brown fur in summer for camouflage.
Stripes help zebras and tigers blend in or confuse predators.
A stick insect looks like a twig and a leaf insect looks like a leaf, which helps them hide from predators.
Mimicry is when a harmless animal looks like a harmful or poisonous one, which protects it from predators.
Bright warning colors tell predators that an animal such as a poison dart frog is poisonous or tastes bad.
Skunks spray a bad-smelling liquid to defend themselves.
Porcupines have sharp quills that protect them from predators.
Turtles and snails have hard shells for protection.
Some animals play dead to avoid predators, like the opossum.
Many fish swim in schools, and many animals live in herds, for protection from predators.
Thick fur and a layer of fat, or blubber, keep animals such as polar bears, seals and whales warm in cold climates.
Polar bears have thick fur, a layer of fat, and large paws to walk on snow and ice.
Desert animals such as camels can survive long periods without water.
Camels store fat in their humps and have long eyelashes to keep out sand.
Many desert animals are nocturnal, active at night when it is cooler, and stay in burrows during the hot day.
Large ears help animals like the fennec fox and jackrabbit release heat in hot deserts.
Small ears help arctic animals keep body heat.
Webbed feet help ducks, frogs and otters swim.
The long neck of a giraffe helps it reach leaves high in trees.
Birds' beaks are shaped for the food they eat.
Hawks and eagles have sharp hooked beaks and sharp talons to catch and tear meat.
Hummingbirds have long thin beaks to sip nectar from flowers.
Seed-eating birds such as finches have short, thick, strong beaks to crack seeds.
Woodpeckers have strong, pointed beaks to drill into wood for insects.
Ducks have flat, wide bills to strain food from water.
Pelicans have pouches in their beaks to scoop fish.
Sharp teeth help carnivores tear meat; flat teeth help herbivores grind plants.
Canine teeth are long and pointed for tearing; molars are flat for grinding.
Owls have large eyes to see at night and soft feathers to fly silently to hunt.
Eyes on the front of the head help predators judge distance; eyes on the sides of the head help prey animals see predators.
Cheetahs are fast runners, which helps them catch prey.
Speed helps prey animals such as deer and gazelles escape from predators.
Long legs help animals run fast.
Claws help animals climb, dig and catch prey.
Moles have strong front claws for digging tunnels.
Gills let fish breathe underwater; lungs let land animals breathe air.
Streamlined bodies help fish, dolphins and penguins swim fast through water.
Fins help fish steer and balance.
Fish have a swim bladder that helps them float at different depths.
Deep-sea fish may produce their own light, called bioluminescence, to attract prey in the dark.
Echolocation lets bats and dolphins find food and objects by bouncing sound off them.
Bats use echolocation to hunt insects at night.
Elephants use their trunks to drink, grab food and smell.
Elephants flap their large ears to cool down.
Beavers have strong front teeth to cut down trees to build dams and lodges.
Spiders spin webs to catch insects.
Hibernation is a deep sleep that helps animals survive the winter when food is scarce.
During hibernation an animal's body temperature, heart rate and breathing slow down to save energy.
Bears, ground squirrels, bats and woodchucks hibernate in winter.
Animals store fat before hibernating to use for energy during winter.
Migration is the seasonal movement of animals from one region to another to find food, warmer weather or a place to reproduce.
Many birds migrate south in the fall to find food and warmer weather, and return north in spring.
Monarch butterflies, whales, caribou, salmon and geese migrate.
Salmon migrate from the ocean up rivers to lay eggs where they hatched.
Migrating birds use the Sun, stars, landmarks and Earth's magnetic field to navigate.
Squirrels store nuts in the fall to eat in winter.
Some animals grow thicker fur in winter and shed it in summer.
Instincts are behaviors animals are born with and do not have to learn, such as spinning a web, building a nest or a baby suckling.
Learned behaviors are behaviors an animal gets from experience or from watching others, such as a dog doing tricks.
Birds building nests and spiders spinning webs are instinctive behaviors.
Training a dog to sit is a learned behavior.
Many animals communicate using sounds, smells, colors and movements.
Honeybees do a dance to tell other bees where to find food.
Ants leave scent trails, chemicals called pheromones, to communicate.
Wolves live in packs and hunt together.
Social insects such as ants, bees and termites live in colonies with a queen and workers who do different jobs.
Courtship displays, such as a peacock showing its colorful tail feathers, attract mates.
Birds sing to attract mates and mark their territory.
Many animals care for their young, feeding and protecting them until they can survive alone.
Animals that lay many eggs, like fish and frogs, usually give little care to their young.
Mammals usually have few young and take care of them for a long time.
Animals that lay eggs on land have eggs with shells that keep them from drying out.
Birds sit on their eggs to keep them warm until they hatch, called incubation.
Sea turtles lay eggs on beaches, and the young crawl to the ocean when they hatch.
Kangaroos and koalas are marsupials; their young develop in a pouch.
Herbivores eat only plants, such as deer, rabbits, cows, horses, giraffes and elephants.
Carnivores eat only other animals, such as lions, tigers, wolves, sharks, hawks, owls and snakes.
Omnivores eat both plants and animals, such as humans, bears, raccoons, pigs and chickens.
Scavengers such as vultures, hyenas and crows eat dead animals.
A predator is an animal that hunts and eats other animals; prey is the animal that is eaten.
Owls are predators of mice; hawks eat rabbits and snakes; foxes eat rabbits.
Frogs and many birds eat insects.
Snakes swallow their prey whole.
A snake smells by flicking its tongue.
Animals get the energy they need by eating food.
Animals get oxygen from air or water and give off carbon dioxide.
Animals need shelter to protect them from weather and predators.
Animals depend on plants for food, oxygen and shelter.
Some animals depend on other animals for food, and some depend on plants to make homes.
Coral reefs are built by tiny animals called corals.
Sponges are simple animals that filter food from water.
Jellyfish have stinging tentacles to catch prey.
Starfish, or sea stars, can regrow lost arms, called regeneration.
Some lizards can lose their tails to escape predators and grow new ones.
Snakes and other reptiles shed their skin as they grow.
Insects such as bees and butterflies pollinate flowers.
Some insects, such as locusts and aphids, damage crops.
Mosquitoes can spread diseases such as malaria.
Ticks and fleas are parasites that feed on the blood of other animals.
Dogs have a strong sense of smell and are used to find people and objects.
Cats have whiskers that help them sense their surroundings in the dark.
Horses, cows and dogs have been domesticated by people.
Selective breeding by people has produced many breeds of dogs, cattle and chickens with desired traits.
Animals inherit their traits from their parents.
Many animals that live in caves have no eyes or poor eyesight and pale skin.
Animals that live in trees, like monkeys, have long arms, grasping hands and tails for climbing.
Feathers keep birds warm and help them fly.
Down feathers trap air to keep birds warm.
Bird wings are shaped to create lift for flying.
Flying squirrels glide using flaps of skin.
Some fish, such as eels, live in both fresh and salt water during their lives.
Sea otters use rocks as tools to crack open shellfish.
Chimpanzees use sticks as tools to get termites out of nests.
Dinosaurs were reptiles that lived millions of years ago and are now extinct.
Birds are thought to have evolved from dinosaurs.
