# Ecosystems, food chains and webs, energy flow, biomes, relationships, populations, succession
An ecosystem is all the living and nonliving things that interact in an area.
Biotic factors are the living parts of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
Abiotic factors are the nonliving parts of an ecosystem, such as sunlight, water, air, temperature, soil and rocks.
A population is all the members of one species living in an area.
A community is all the populations of different species living and interacting in an area.
A habitat is the place where an organism lives and gets the food, water, shelter and space it needs.
A niche is the role an organism plays in its ecosystem, including what it eats and what eats it.
The Sun is the source of energy for almost all ecosystems.
Energy flows through an ecosystem from the Sun to producers to consumers.
A food chain shows how energy passes from one organism to another as they eat.
The arrows in a food chain or food web point in the direction energy flows, from the organism eaten to the organism that eats it.
A food chain starts with a producer, usually a green plant, which gets energy from the Sun.
A food web is many connected food chains in an ecosystem.
Producers, such as plants and algae, make their own food using energy from sunlight.
Consumers get energy by eating other organisms.
Primary consumers are herbivores that eat producers.
Secondary consumers eat primary consumers; tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers.
Top predators are at the top of the food chain and have few or no natural predators.
Decomposers such as bacteria, fungi and earthworms break down dead plants and animals and wastes.
Decomposers return nutrients to the soil so plants can use them again.
Without decomposers, dead organisms would pile up and nutrients would not be recycled.
Mushrooms and molds are decomposers.
An example food chain is grass, grasshopper, frog, snake, hawk.
An example food chain is grass, rabbit, fox.
An example ocean food chain is phytoplankton, zooplankton, small fish, large fish, shark.
An example food chain is seeds, mouse, snake, owl.
An energy pyramid shows that energy decreases at each level of a food chain.
Only about 10 percent of the energy at one level of a food chain is passed to the next level.
Most energy at each level is used for life processes or lost as heat.
Producers make up the largest level of an energy pyramid, with the most energy.
There are fewer organisms at the top of a food chain because less energy is available.
Matter, such as carbon, nitrogen and water, is recycled in ecosystems, but energy flows through and is not recycled.
In the carbon cycle, plants take in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis, and organisms release carbon dioxide by respiration and decay.
Burning fossil fuels releases carbon stored for millions of years into the atmosphere as carbon dioxide.
In the nitrogen cycle, bacteria change nitrogen gas into compounds that plants can use.
Oxygen and carbon dioxide cycle between plants and animals through photosynthesis and respiration.
If a population of predators decreases, the population of its prey usually increases.
If the prey population increases, the predator population may increase because there is more food.
If a predator is removed from an ecosystem, the prey population may grow too large and overgraze the plants.
If a producer is removed, the herbivores that eat it decrease, and so do the animals that eat them.
Removing one species can affect many other species in a food web.
Predator and prey populations rise and fall in cycles.
Limiting factors such as food, water, space, shelter, disease and predators limit the size of a population.
Carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support.
Competition happens when organisms need the same limited resources, such as food, water, light or space.
Organisms compete for resources within a species and between species.
Symbiosis is a close relationship between two different species.
Mutualism is a relationship in which both species benefit, such as bees and flowers.
Commensalism is a relationship in which one species benefits and the other is not helped or harmed, such as barnacles on a whale or birds nesting in trees.
Parasitism is a relationship in which one organism, the parasite, benefits and the host is harmed, such as ticks, fleas, tapeworms and mistletoe.
Clownfish and sea anemones help each other, an example of mutualism.
Lichens are a mutualism between fungi and algae.
Oxpecker birds eat ticks off zebras and rhinos, a mutualism.
Invasive species are organisms brought to a new area where they have no natural predators, so they spread and harm native species.
Zebra mussels, kudzu, cane toads and starlings are invasive species.
Native species are species that naturally live in an area.
A keystone species has a large effect on its ecosystem; beavers and sea otters are keystone species.
Beavers build dams that create ponds and change the habitat for many other organisms.
Animals change their environment: beavers build dams, earthworms loosen soil, and elephants knock down trees.
Humans change ecosystems by building cities, farming, cutting forests, polluting and introducing species.
A biome is a large region with a similar climate, plants and animals.
Major land biomes include tundra, taiga, deciduous forest, tropical rain forest, grassland and desert.
The tundra is a cold, treeless biome with permafrost, frozen ground under the surface, and short growing seasons.
Tundra plants are short, such as mosses and lichens, and animals include caribou, arctic foxes and snowy owls.
The taiga, or boreal forest, has long cold winters and coniferous trees such as pine, spruce and fir.
Temperate deciduous forests have four seasons and trees such as oak and maple that lose their leaves in fall.
Tropical rain forests are hot and wet all year and have the greatest biodiversity of any land biome.
Rain forests have many layers, from the forest floor to the canopy.
Grasslands, prairies and savannas have grasses, few trees, and grazing animals.
Deserts are dry biomes with less than 25 centimeters of rain a year; plants and animals are adapted to save water.
Aquatic ecosystems include freshwater lakes, ponds, rivers and wetlands, and saltwater oceans, coral reefs and estuaries.
An estuary is where fresh water from a river mixes with salt water from the ocean.
Wetlands such as marshes and swamps filter water, reduce flooding and provide habitat for many organisms.
Coral reefs are ocean ecosystems with great biodiversity, found in warm, shallow, clear water.
Most ocean life lives near the surface where sunlight reaches.
Sunlight does not reach the deep ocean, so there are no plants there.
Ecological succession is the gradual change in the species living in an area over time.
Primary succession begins on bare rock where there is no soil, such as after a volcanic eruption or a glacier retreats.
Lichens and mosses are pioneer species, the first organisms to grow on bare rock; they help form soil.
Secondary succession happens where soil remains after a disturbance such as a forest fire, flood or abandoned farm field.
After a forest fire, grasses and small plants grow first, then shrubs, then trees.
A climax community is a stable community at the end of succession.
Some forest fires help ecosystems by clearing dead plants and releasing nutrients; some seeds need fire to open.
Natural disasters such as floods, fires, droughts and volcanic eruptions change ecosystems.
A healthy ecosystem has biodiversity, many different species.
Ecosystems with more biodiversity are more stable and recover from changes more easily.
Animals that eat many kinds of food are more likely to survive when one food source disappears.
Plants provide food and shelter for animals, and animals help plants by pollinating flowers and spreading seeds.
Bacteria and other microorganisms in the soil decompose matter and recycle nutrients for all organisms in the ecosystem.
All the organisms in an ecosystem depend on producers and decomposers.
Scavengers and decomposers clean up dead material in ecosystems.
Organisms in an ecosystem depend on each other for food, shelter, pollination and seed dispersal.
A change in temperature or rainfall can change which organisms can live in an ecosystem.
When a habitat is destroyed, organisms must move, adapt or die.
Habitat loss is the biggest threat to most endangered species.
Endangered species are species with so few members that they are at risk of becoming extinct.
Protecting habitats, creating wildlife refuges and laws against hunting help endangered species.
The bald eagle population recovered after the pesticide DDT was banned.
DDT built up in food chains and made eagle and hawk eggshells thin, a process called biomagnification.
Toxins become more concentrated in animals higher in the food chain.
Overfishing reduces fish populations.
Pollution of water can kill fish and other aquatic organisms.
Fertilizer runoff causes algae to grow quickly in lakes, using up oxygen so fish die.
Oil spills harm birds, fish and other ocean life.
Population growth of humans increases the demand for food, water, energy and land.
Organisms in a pond ecosystem include algae, water plants, insects, frogs, fish, turtles and bacteria.
In a forest ecosystem, trees are producers, deer and insects are consumers, and fungi are decomposers.
Producers in the ocean are mostly algae and phytoplankton.
A terrarium or aquarium is a model of an ecosystem.
Light, temperature, water and soil determine what plants grow in an area, and plants determine what animals live there.
Seasonal changes affect organisms: some migrate, some hibernate, and some plants become dormant.
Animals that live in the same area and eat the same food compete with each other.
Grazing animals such as cattle can overgraze grasslands, causing erosion.
Food chains on land usually begin with green plants; food chains in the ocean begin with algae.
The energy in food a person eats originally came from the Sun.
When a rabbit eats grass, energy from the grass is transferred to the rabbit.
An owl that eats a mouse that ate seeds gets energy that came originally from the Sun.
