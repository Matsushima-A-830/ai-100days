# Rocks, minerals, soil, weathering, erosion, plate tectonics, earthquakes, volcanoes, fossils, Earth history
Earth has layers: the crust, the mantle, the outer core and the inner core.
The crust is the thin, solid, rocky outer layer of Earth.
The crust is the thinnest layer of Earth; oceanic crust is thinner and denser than continental crust.
The mantle is the thickest layer of Earth, made of hot rock that flows slowly.
The outer core is made of liquid iron and nickel.
The inner core is solid iron and nickel because of the enormous pressure, even though it is extremely hot.
The inner core is the hottest layer of Earth.
Temperature and pressure increase with depth inside Earth.
The lithosphere is the rigid outer layer made of the crust and upper mantle, broken into tectonic plates.
The asthenosphere is the soft, partly melted layer of the upper mantle on which the plates move.
Earth's magnetic field is produced by the movement of liquid iron in the outer core.
Scientists learn about Earth's interior by studying seismic waves from earthquakes.
The theory of plate tectonics states that Earth's lithosphere is broken into large plates that slowly move.
Tectonic plates move a few centimeters each year, about as fast as fingernails grow.
Convection currents in the mantle cause the tectonic plates to move.
Heat from Earth's interior drives convection currents in the mantle.
Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift, the idea that the continents were once joined and have moved apart.
Evidence for continental drift includes matching coastlines, similar fossils on different continents, matching rock layers and evidence of ancient climates.
The coastlines of South America and Africa fit together like puzzle pieces.
Pangaea was a supercontinent that broke apart about 200 million years ago.
Fossils of the same land animals found in South America and Africa show the continents were once connected.
At a divergent boundary, plates move apart and new crust forms, as at mid-ocean ridges.
Seafloor spreading at mid-ocean ridges creates new ocean crust as magma rises and cools.
The youngest rocks of the ocean floor are near the mid-ocean ridge, and rocks get older farther from the ridge.
At a convergent boundary, plates move toward each other and collide.
When an oceanic plate collides with a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate sinks beneath it in a subduction zone.
Subduction zones form deep ocean trenches and volcanoes.
When two continental plates collide, the crust is pushed up to form tall mountain ranges such as the Himalayas.
The Himalaya Mountains formed when the Indian plate collided with the Eurasian plate.
At a transform boundary, plates slide past each other, causing earthquakes, as along the San Andreas Fault in California.
Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along plate boundaries.
The Ring of Fire is a zone of many volcanoes and earthquakes around the edges of the Pacific Ocean.
An earthquake is a sudden shaking of the ground caused by the release of energy when rocks break and move along a fault.
A fault is a crack in Earth's crust along which rocks move.
The focus of an earthquake is the point underground where it begins; the epicenter is the point on the surface directly above it.
Earthquakes send out seismic waves that travel through Earth.
A seismograph is an instrument that measures and records seismic waves from earthquakes.
The Richter scale and the moment magnitude scale measure the energy, or magnitude, of earthquakes.
Primary waves, or P waves, are the fastest seismic waves and travel through solids and liquids.
Secondary waves, or S waves, cannot travel through liquids, which shows that the outer core is liquid.
Scientists use data from three seismograph stations to locate the epicenter of an earthquake by triangulation.
An earthquake under the ocean can cause a tsunami, a series of giant ocean waves.
Earthquakes can cause landslides, building collapse and tsunamis.
Buildings in earthquake zones are designed to bend and sway so they do not collapse.
A volcano is an opening in Earth's crust where magma, gas and ash erupt.
Magma is melted rock below Earth's surface; lava is melted rock that reaches the surface.
Volcanic eruptions can release ash, lava, gases and rock into the air.
Volcanic ash in the atmosphere can block sunlight and cool the climate for a time.
Volcanoes can form new land, such as islands.
The Hawaiian Islands formed from volcanoes over a hot spot in the middle of the Pacific plate.
Shield volcanoes have gentle slopes built from runny lava flows; composite volcanoes are steep cones of lava and ash layers.
Cinder cone volcanoes are small, steep volcanoes made of ash and cinders.
Volcanic soil is often very fertile and good for farming.
Mountains can form by plates colliding, by folding and faulting of rock, and by volcanoes.
Folding happens when rock layers bend because of pressure from moving plates.
Uplift raises land to higher elevations.
Constructive forces build up landforms; destructive forces such as weathering and erosion wear landforms down.
Volcanoes, earthquakes and deposition are constructive forces; weathering and erosion are destructive forces.
A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and a crystal structure.
Minerals are identified by properties such as hardness, luster, streak, color, cleavage, fracture and density.
Hardness is how easily a mineral can be scratched.
The hardness of a mineral is tested by scratching it, and is ranked on the Mohs hardness scale from 1 to 10.
On the Mohs scale, talc is the softest mineral at 1 and diamond is the hardest at 10.
A mineral can scratch any mineral that is softer than it.
Quartz has a hardness of 7 and can scratch glass.
A fingernail has a hardness of about 2.5, a copper penny about 3.5, and a steel nail or glass about 5.5.
Luster describes how the surface of a mineral reflects light, such as metallic, glassy or dull.
Luster can be observed by looking at a mineral.
Streak is the color of the powder a mineral leaves when it is rubbed on a white unglazed tile, the streak plate.
The streak of a mineral is more reliable than its color for identification because color can vary.
Color is the least reliable property for identifying a mineral because many minerals come in different colors.
Cleavage is the tendency of a mineral to split along flat surfaces; fracture is breaking unevenly.
Mica has cleavage and splits into thin flat sheets.
Magnetite is a mineral that is magnetic.
Calcite fizzes and bubbles when a drop of acid such as vinegar is put on it.
Halite is the mineral name for rock salt; it tastes salty and forms cubic crystals.
Crystals form when minerals cool from magma or when water evaporates from a solution.
Large crystals form when magma cools slowly; small crystals form when lava cools quickly.
A rock is a naturally formed solid made of one or more minerals.
There are three main types of rock: igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic.
Igneous rocks form when magma or lava cools and hardens.
Intrusive igneous rocks such as granite cool slowly underground and have large crystals.
Extrusive igneous rocks such as basalt and obsidian cool quickly at the surface and have small crystals or no crystals.
Obsidian is volcanic glass that cools so fast that no crystals form.
Pumice is a light igneous rock with many holes from gas bubbles; it can float on water.
Granite is an igneous rock with large crystals of quartz and feldspar.
Basalt is a dark, fine-grained igneous rock formed from lava; it makes up most of the ocean floor.
Sedimentary rocks form when layers of sediment are pressed and cemented together over time.
Sediments are pieces of rock, sand, mud, shells and remains of organisms deposited by water, wind or ice.
Sedimentary rock forms through weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction and cementation.
Compaction and cementation turn sediments into sedimentary rock.
Sandstone is a sedimentary rock made of sand grains cemented together.
Shale is a sedimentary rock made of compacted clay and mud.
Limestone is a sedimentary rock formed mostly from shells and skeletons of sea organisms, made of calcite.
Conglomerate is a sedimentary rock with rounded pebbles cemented together.
Coal is a sedimentary rock formed from the remains of ancient plants in swamps.
Fossils are usually found in sedimentary rock.
Sedimentary rocks often show layers, called strata.
Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are changed by heat and pressure deep underground without melting.
Marble is a metamorphic rock formed from limestone.
Slate is a metamorphic rock formed from shale.
Quartzite is a metamorphic rock formed from sandstone.
Gneiss is a metamorphic rock with bands, formed from granite.
The rock cycle shows how rocks change from one type to another over time.
In the rock cycle any type of rock can become another type through melting, weathering, erosion, heat and pressure.
Melting rock forms magma, which cools to form igneous rock.
Weathering is the breaking down of rocks into smaller pieces.
Weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces; erosion moves those pieces to new places.
Mechanical, or physical, weathering breaks rock apart without changing what it is made of.
Ice wedging is mechanical weathering in which water freezes in cracks, expands and breaks rocks apart.
When water freezes it expands, so freezing and thawing can crack rocks and roads and form potholes.
Plant roots growing in cracks can break rocks apart.
Abrasion is wearing away of rock by grinding from sand, water or ice.
Chemical weathering changes the minerals in rock, as when rain, acid or oxygen reacts with rock.
Acid rain causes chemical weathering of rock and buildings, especially limestone and marble statues.
Rusting of iron minerals in rocks is chemical weathering by oxygen.
Water dissolving limestone forms caves, which is chemical weathering.
Lichens and mosses produce acids that slowly break down rock.
Chemical weathering is fastest in warm, wet climates.
Erosion is the movement of rock and soil from one place to another by water, wind, ice or gravity.
Water is the main cause of erosion on Earth.
Running water in rivers and streams carries sediment and erodes land, carving valleys and canyons.
The Grand Canyon was carved by erosion from the Colorado River over millions of years.
Rivers form V-shaped valleys; glaciers carve U-shaped valleys.
Glaciers are huge masses of moving ice that erode land, carry rocks and leave behind deposits called moraines.
Glaciers carry boulders and drop them far away when the ice melts.
Glaciers can scratch and polish rocks as they move.
Ocean waves erode beaches and cliffs along the shore.
Wind erosion carries sand and dust, especially in deserts, and forms sand dunes.
Wind blowing sand against rocks wears them down by abrasion.
Gravity causes landslides, mudslides and rockfalls, moving rock and soil downhill.
Floods carry soil particles downstream.
Deposition is the dropping of sediment carried by water, wind or ice in a new place.
When a river slows down, it deposits sediment.
A delta forms where a river flows into an ocean or lake and deposits sediment.
Sand dunes are deposits of sand made by wind.
Plant roots hold soil in place and help prevent erosion.
Planting trees, grass and cover crops helps prevent soil erosion.
Terraces, contour plowing, windbreaks and cover crops help farmers reduce soil erosion.
Removing plants and trees, as in deforestation, increases soil erosion.
Soil is a mixture of weathered rock, minerals, organic matter called humus, water and air.
Soil forms from weathered rock mixed with decayed plants and animals, over a very long time.
Humus is dark organic material in soil made from decayed plants and animals; it adds nutrients.
Decomposers such as bacteria, fungi and earthworms break down dead matter and add nutrients to soil.
Earthworms loosen soil and create spaces for air and water.
Topsoil is the top layer of soil and has the most humus and nutrients.
Sand particles are the largest soil particles, silt particles are medium, and clay particles are the smallest.
Sandy soil drains water quickly; clay soil holds water and drains slowly.
Loam is a mixture of sand, silt, clay and humus that is best for growing most plants.
Soil that holds the most water is clay soil.
A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of an organism that lived long ago.
Fossils form when organisms are buried quickly in sediment that later hardens into rock.
Hard parts like bones, shells and teeth are most likely to become fossils.
A mold fossil is an imprint left in rock; a cast fossil forms when minerals fill a mold.
Trace fossils such as footprints, tracks and burrows show how ancient animals behaved.
Petrified wood forms when minerals replace the wood of a buried tree.
Some insects were preserved whole in amber, which is hardened tree sap.
Some animals such as mammoths were preserved frozen in ice.
Fossils give evidence about organisms that lived in the past and how environments changed.
Fossils of sea animals found on land or in mountains show that the area was once covered by an ocean.
Fossils of tropical plants found in cold places show that the climate there was once warm.
Fossils show that many species that lived in the past are now extinct.
The fossil record shows that life on Earth has changed over time.
The law of superposition states that in undisturbed rock layers the oldest layers are at the bottom and the youngest are at the top.
Fossils in lower rock layers are usually older than fossils in upper layers.
Index fossils are fossils of organisms that lived for a short time over a wide area; they help date rock layers.
Relative dating tells which rock layer is older; absolute dating gives the age in years.
Radioactive dating uses the decay of radioactive elements to find the absolute age of rocks and fossils.
Carbon-14 dating is used to find the age of once-living things up to about 50,000 years old.
A half-life is the time it takes for half of the atoms of a radioactive element to decay.
The geologic time scale divides Earth's history into eras and periods based on fossils and events.
Dinosaurs lived during the Mesozoic Era and became extinct about 66 million years ago.
An asteroid impact is thought to have caused the extinction of the dinosaurs.
The first living things on Earth were simple single-celled organisms like bacteria.
Most of Earth's history happened before complex animals appeared.
Humans appeared very recently in Earth's history.
A landform is a natural feature of Earth's surface such as a mountain, valley, plain, plateau, canyon or delta.
A plateau is a high, flat area of land.
A plain is a large flat area of land at low elevation.
A canyon is a deep, narrow valley with steep sides, usually carved by a river.
A topographic map uses contour lines to show elevation and the shape of the land.
Contour lines that are close together show a steep slope; lines far apart show a gentle slope.
A map key or legend explains the symbols on a map, and a map scale shows distances.
Latitude lines run east and west and measure distance north or south of the equator.
Longitude lines run north and south and measure distance east or west of the prime meridian.
A compass is used to find direction.
A globe is a model of Earth.
Groundwater is water that soaks into the ground and fills spaces between rocks and soil.
An aquifer is an underground layer of rock or sediment that holds groundwater.
Wells draw groundwater from aquifers.
Permeable rock and soil let water pass through; impermeable materials such as clay do not.
Caves often form in limestone when groundwater dissolves the rock.
Stalactites hang from cave ceilings and stalagmites grow up from cave floors as minerals are deposited by dripping water.
A sinkhole forms when the ground collapses into an underground space dissolved by water.
Ores are rocks that contain useful metals or minerals that can be mined.
Iron ore, copper ore and aluminum ore called bauxite are mined to obtain metals.
Gems such as diamonds, rubies and emeralds are valuable minerals.
Graphite and diamond are both forms of carbon.
Gold is a soft metal mineral that does not rust or tarnish.
Quartz is one of the most common minerals in Earth's crust.
Oxygen and silicon are the most common elements in Earth's crust.
Iron is the most common element in Earth's core.
Geysers erupt hot water and steam heated by magma underground, like Old Faithful in Yellowstone.
Hot springs are heated by geothermal energy from inside Earth.
Rocks found at the bottom of a river are usually smooth and rounded because water and sediment wore them down.
Rocks broken by ice wedging or freshly broken rocks have sharp edges.
Sand on a beach comes from rocks and shells broken down by waves.
A geologist is a scientist who studies rocks, minerals and the history of Earth.
A paleontologist is a scientist who studies fossils.
A seismologist studies earthquakes.
A volcanologist studies volcanoes.
The movement of plates over millions of years changes the positions of continents and oceans.
Mountains that are old have been worn down by erosion and are rounded, like the Appalachian Mountains.
Young mountains like the Rocky Mountains and Himalayas are tall with sharp peaks.
Fast changes to Earth's surface include earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, landslides and floods.
Slow changes to Earth's surface include weathering, erosion, deposition and mountain building.
