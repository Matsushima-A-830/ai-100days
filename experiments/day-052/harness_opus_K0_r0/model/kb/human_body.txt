# Human body systems, health, disease, nutrition
The human body is made of systems that work together, such as the circulatory, respiratory, digestive, nervous, skeletal and muscular systems.
The circulatory system moves blood through the body, carrying oxygen and nutrients to cells and removing wastes.
The circulatory system includes the heart, blood and blood vessels.
The heart is a muscle that pumps blood through the body.
The heart has four chambers: two atria and two ventricles.
Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood back to the heart.
Capillaries are tiny blood vessels where oxygen, nutrients and wastes are exchanged with cells.
Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to the cells of the body.
Hemoglobin in red blood cells carries oxygen; iron is needed to make hemoglobin.
White blood cells fight infection and help protect the body from disease.
Platelets help blood clot to stop bleeding when you are cut.
Plasma is the liquid part of blood that carries nutrients, hormones and wastes.
Heart rate and breathing rate increase during exercise to deliver more oxygen to muscles.
During exercise the heart beats faster to pump more blood and oxygen to the muscles.
Your pulse is the beating of your heart that you can feel in arteries in your wrist or neck.
The respiratory system takes in oxygen and gets rid of carbon dioxide.
The respiratory system includes the nose, trachea or windpipe, bronchi, lungs and diaphragm.
In the lungs, oxygen passes into the blood and carbon dioxide passes out of the blood.
Alveoli are tiny air sacs in the lungs where gases are exchanged with the blood.
The diaphragm is a muscle below the lungs that helps you breathe.
When you inhale, the diaphragm contracts and moves down, and air enters the lungs.
We breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide.
The respiratory and circulatory systems work together to deliver oxygen to cells and remove carbon dioxide.
Smoking damages the lungs and can cause lung cancer and heart disease.
Asthma narrows the airways and makes breathing difficult.
The digestive system breaks food down into nutrients that the body can absorb and use.
Digestion begins in the mouth, where teeth chew food and saliva begins breaking down starch.
The esophagus is a tube that carries food from the mouth to the stomach.
The stomach uses acid and enzymes to break down food.
Most digestion and absorption of nutrients happens in the small intestine.
Villi in the small intestine increase surface area to absorb nutrients into the blood.
The large intestine absorbs water from undigested food.
The liver makes bile that helps digest fats, and it filters harmful substances from the blood.
The pancreas makes digestive enzymes and the hormone insulin.
The digestive system and circulatory system work together: nutrients absorbed in the intestine are carried by blood to cells.
The excretory system removes liquid wastes from the body.
The kidneys filter wastes from the blood and make urine.
Urine is stored in the bladder.
The skin, lungs and kidneys all remove wastes from the body.
Sweating removes wastes and helps cool the body.
The nervous system controls and coordinates the body and sends messages between the brain and the body.
The nervous system includes the brain, spinal cord and nerves.
The brain is the control center of the body.
The spinal cord carries messages between the brain and the rest of the body.
Nerves carry signals from the sense organs to the brain and from the brain to the muscles.
Neurons are nerve cells that carry electrical signals.
A reflex is a quick automatic response that does not need the brain to think, like pulling your hand away from a hot stove.
The cerebrum controls thinking, memory and the senses; the cerebellum controls balance and coordination.
The brain stem controls automatic functions like breathing and heart rate.
The five senses are sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch.
The eyes detect light, the ears detect sound, the nose detects smells, the tongue detects tastes and the skin detects touch, pressure, heat and pain.
The iris controls the amount of light that enters the eye by changing the size of the pupil.
The pupil gets smaller in bright light and larger in dim light.
The lens of the eye focuses light on the retina.
The retina contains cells that detect light and send signals through the optic nerve to the brain.
The eardrum vibrates when sound waves hit it.
Sound vibrations travel through the eardrum and small bones to the inner ear, where nerves send signals to the brain.
The inner ear also helps you keep your balance.
The skeletal system supports the body, protects organs, helps the body move, and makes blood cells in bone marrow.
The human skeleton has 206 bones.
The skull protects the brain.
The rib cage protects the heart and lungs.
The backbone, or spine, is made of vertebrae and protects the spinal cord.
Joints are places where two bones meet; they allow the body to move.
Ligaments connect bones to bones; tendons connect muscles to bones.
Cartilage is a flexible tissue that cushions joints and shapes the nose and ears.
Calcium and vitamin D help keep bones strong.
The muscular system moves the body by contracting muscles.
Muscles work in pairs: when one muscle contracts, the other relaxes.
Skeletal muscles move bones and are voluntary; you control them.
Smooth muscles in organs such as the stomach and blood vessels are involuntary.
Cardiac muscle is found only in the heart and works without stopping.
The skeletal and muscular systems work together to move the body.
The integumentary system includes the skin, hair and nails.
The skin is the largest organ of the body; it protects the body from germs, injury and water loss and helps control temperature.
The skin is the body's first line of defense against germs.
Sunscreen protects the skin from ultraviolet rays that can cause sunburn and skin cancer.
The endocrine system uses glands to make hormones, chemical messengers carried in the blood to control body functions.
Hormones travel through the bloodstream and carry messages to cells and organs.
Glands such as the pituitary, thyroid and adrenal glands make hormones.
Insulin is a hormone that controls the amount of sugar in the blood; diabetes occurs when the body cannot control blood sugar.
Adrenaline prepares the body for fight or flight in an emergency.
The immune system protects the body from disease-causing germs, called pathogens.
White blood cells and antibodies attack germs such as bacteria and viruses.
Vaccines help the body build immunity by causing it to make antibodies against a disease without getting sick.
Vaccines prevent diseases such as measles, polio and flu.
Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria; they do not work on viruses like colds and flu.
Infectious diseases are caused by pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites and can spread from person to person.
Washing hands with soap and water is one of the best ways to prevent the spread of germs and disease.
Covering your mouth when you cough or sneeze helps stop the spread of germs.
Germs can spread through the air, through touch, through food and water, and through insect bites.
Noninfectious diseases such as heart disease, diabetes and cancer are not spread from person to person.
Louis Pasteur showed that germs cause disease and invented pasteurization, heating milk to kill bacteria.
Edward Jenner developed the first vaccine, against smallpox.
Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin.
The reproductive system produces offspring; in humans the egg from the mother and the sperm from the father join at fertilization.
A fertilized egg develops into an embryo and then a fetus inside the mother's uterus.
Puberty is the time when the body matures and becomes able to reproduce.
The human life cycle includes infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood and old age.
Nutrients are substances in food that the body needs for energy, growth and repair: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and water.
Carbohydrates such as bread, pasta, rice and potatoes are the body's main source of energy.
Sugars and starches are carbohydrates.
Proteins in meat, fish, eggs, beans, nuts and milk build and repair muscles and tissues.
Fats provide stored energy, but too much fat can lead to heart disease.
Vitamins and minerals keep the body healthy.
Vitamin C from citrus fruits like oranges prevents scurvy and helps the immune system.
Calcium from milk, cheese and yogurt builds strong bones and teeth.
Iron from meat and leafy green vegetables is needed for healthy blood.
Fiber from fruits, vegetables and whole grains helps digestion.
Water is needed for all body functions; the human body is mostly water.
A balanced diet includes fruits, vegetables, grains, protein foods and dairy.
Eating a balanced diet, exercising regularly, and getting enough sleep keep the body healthy.
Regular exercise strengthens the heart, lungs, muscles and bones.
Food gives the body energy, measured in Calories.
The body needs more energy during exercise.
Junk food high in sugar and fat has few nutrients.
Brushing teeth and flossing prevent tooth decay caused by bacteria.
Sleep allows the body and brain to rest and repair.
Drugs, alcohol and tobacco harm the body.
Wearing a helmet protects the head and brain from injury.
Body temperature of a healthy person is about 37 degrees Celsius, or 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit.
A fever is a higher than normal body temperature that helps the body fight infection.
Shivering produces heat when the body is cold; sweating cools the body when it is hot.
When sweat evaporates from the skin it cools the body.
Blood vessels in the skin widen when you are hot to release heat.
Traits such as eye color, hair color and blood type are inherited from parents.
Doctors use X-rays to see bones inside the body.
A stethoscope is used to listen to the heart and lungs.
Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of arteries.
Teeth are covered by enamel, the hardest substance in the body.
Incisors cut food, canines tear food and molars grind food.
Saliva moistens food and contains enzymes that begin to digest starch.
Allergies are reactions of the immune system to harmless substances such as pollen.
Organ systems depend on each other; for example, the muscles need oxygen from the respiratory system and food from the digestive system delivered by the circulatory system.
