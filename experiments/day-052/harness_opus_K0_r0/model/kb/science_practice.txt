# Scientific method, investigations, lab safety, tools, measurement, data, models, engineering design
The scientific method includes asking a question, doing research, forming a hypothesis, testing with an experiment, analyzing data and drawing a conclusion.
A hypothesis is a testable prediction or possible explanation that can be tested by an experiment.
A hypothesis is often written as an if-then statement.
A prediction is a statement about what will happen in the future based on observations or evidence.
An observation is information gathered using the senses or instruments.
A qualitative observation describes qualities such as color, smell or texture; a quantitative observation uses numbers and measurements.
An inference is an explanation or conclusion based on observations and prior knowledge.
A conclusion is a statement that tells whether the data support the hypothesis.
A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation supported by a large body of evidence.
A scientific law describes what happens in nature, often with a mathematical rule, but does not explain why.
Scientific knowledge can change when new evidence is discovered.
Scientists share their results so other scientists can repeat and check their experiments.
Scientists publish their results so other scientists can review them.
Scientists repeat experiments several times to make sure the results are reliable and accurate.
Repeating trials and using a large sample size makes results more reliable.
Replication means other scientists repeat an experiment and get the same results.
A controlled experiment tests only one variable at a time.
The independent variable is the factor the scientist changes on purpose; it is also called the manipulated variable.
The dependent variable is the factor that is measured; it changes in response to the independent variable; it is also called the responding variable.
Controlled variables, or constants, are factors kept the same in all groups of an experiment.
The control group does not receive the treatment being tested and is used for comparison.
The experimental group receives the treatment being tested.
To make a fair test, change only one variable and keep all other conditions the same.
If more than one variable is changed, the scientist cannot tell which variable caused the result.
In an experiment on plant growth with fertilizer, the amount of fertilizer is the independent variable and the plant height is the dependent variable.
When testing plants, use the same kind of plant, the same soil, the same amount of water and the same light for all groups.
Data are facts, numbers and observations collected during an experiment.
Data should be recorded accurately in a data table or science notebook as they are collected.
Scientists record all data honestly, even if the results do not support the hypothesis.
If the results do not support the hypothesis, the scientist should revise the hypothesis and test again.
Graphs help show patterns and relationships in data.
A line graph shows how something changes over time or how one variable changes another.
A bar graph compares amounts in different categories.
A circle graph, or pie chart, shows parts of a whole as percentages.
On a graph, the independent variable is usually on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis.
A trend is a pattern in data.
Averaging the results of several trials gives a more reliable result.
Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value; precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other.
Error can come from measuring tools, mistakes or uncontrolled variables.
A model is a representation of an object or process, such as a globe, a map, a diagram or a computer simulation.
Models help scientists study things that are too big, too small, too far away, too slow, too fast or too dangerous to study directly.
Models have limitations because they are simpler than the real thing.
Scientists use computer models to predict weather and climate.
A scientific question can be answered by observations, experiments and evidence.
Questions about opinions, beliefs or what is beautiful cannot be answered by science.
Evidence is information from observations and data that supports or disproves an idea.
Scientists should be skeptical, open-minded, curious, honest and willing to change their ideas based on evidence.
Scientists work in teams and communicate with each other.
Peer review is when other scientists check research before it is published.
Bias can affect results; a good experiment avoids bias.
A sample should be large and random to represent the whole population.
Lab safety rules: wear safety goggles, follow the teacher's directions, never eat or drink in the lab, and report accidents and spills.
Safety goggles protect the eyes from chemicals, heat, flying objects and splashes.
Students should wear goggles when heating substances or working with chemicals or glass.
Gloves protect hands from chemicals and from bacteria in samples.
A lab apron protects clothing and skin from spills.
Never eat, drink or taste anything in a laboratory.
Bringing food and drinks into a laboratory is unsafe.
Students should wash their hands after handling chemicals, plants, animals or samples, and before leaving the laboratory.
Tie back long hair and roll up loose sleeves when working near flames.
Never point the open end of a heated test tube at yourself or others.
Smell chemicals by wafting the fumes toward your nose with your hand, never by sniffing directly.
Tell the teacher immediately if there is an accident, spill, broken glass or injury.
Broken glass should be swept up with a brush and dustpan, not picked up with hands.
Dispose of chemicals as directed by the teacher, not down the drain unless instructed.
Know the location of the fire extinguisher, eye wash station and safety shower.
If a chemical gets in your eyes, rinse them with water at the eyewash station for several minutes.
Use tongs or heat-resistant gloves to handle hot objects.
Clean materials and the work area after an experiment.
Treat living organisms in experiments carefully and humanely.
Samples of bacteria should be kept covered and handled with gloves.
A hand lens, or magnifying glass, makes small objects look larger.
A microscope is used to observe very small things such as cells.
A telescope is used to observe distant objects such as stars and planets.
Binoculars help observe distant birds and animals.
A balance, such as a triple beam balance or electronic scale, measures mass in grams or kilograms.
A spring scale measures force and weight in newtons.
A graduated cylinder measures the volume of liquids in milliliters.
A beaker holds and mixes liquids but gives only rough volume measurements.
A ruler or meter stick measures length in centimeters and meters.
A metric ruler is used to measure small lengths in millimeters and centimeters.
A tape measure measures long distances.
A thermometer measures temperature in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit.
A stopwatch or timer measures time in seconds.
A pH meter or litmus paper measures acidity.
A barometer measures air pressure, an anemometer measures wind speed, and a rain gauge measures rainfall.
A compass shows direction.
A seismograph records earthquakes.
A Bunsen burner or hot plate is used to heat substances.
Test tubes hold small amounts of chemicals.
An eyedropper or pipette transfers small amounts of liquid.
Petri dishes are used to grow bacteria and fungi.
Forceps or tweezers pick up small objects.
The metric system, or International System of Units, is used by scientists around the world.
The meter is the basic unit of length, the gram is the basic unit of mass, and the liter is the basic unit of volume.
One kilometer equals 1,000 meters; one meter equals 100 centimeters; one centimeter equals 10 millimeters.
One kilogram equals 1,000 grams; one liter equals 1,000 milliliters.
One milliliter equals one cubic centimeter.
Temperature in science is measured in degrees Celsius; water freezes at 0 and boils at 100 degrees Celsius.
Time is measured in seconds, minutes, hours, days and years.
The newton is the unit of force; the joule is the unit of energy and work; the watt is the unit of power.
Speed is measured in meters per second or kilometers per hour.
Density is measured in grams per cubic centimeter or grams per milliliter.
Very large distances in space are measured in light-years or astronomical units.
An astronomical unit is the average distance from Earth to the Sun.
Technology is the use of scientific knowledge to solve problems and make tools and products.
Engineers design solutions to problems using the engineering design process.
The engineering design process includes defining the problem, brainstorming, designing, building a prototype, testing, and improving the design.
A prototype is an early model of a design that is built to be tested.
Criteria are the requirements a design must meet; constraints are limits such as cost, time, materials and safety.
Engineers test designs and redesign them to make improvements.
Engineers choose materials based on their properties, such as strength, weight, cost and flexibility.
Inventions such as the telescope, microscope and computer have helped scientists make new discoveries.
The invention of the microscope led to the discovery of cells and microorganisms.
The telescope allowed scientists to study the Moon, planets and stars in detail.
Computers help scientists collect, store and analyze large amounts of data.
Satellites help scientists study weather, oceans, land and climate.
Advances in technology, such as refrigeration and transportation, have increased the variety of food available.
Improvements in shipping foods around the world have increased the types of food available for sale in stores.
Refrigeration keeps food fresh longer by slowing the growth of bacteria.
Canning, freezing, drying and salting preserve food by preventing the growth of microorganisms.
Technology can have both positive and negative effects on society and the environment.
New technologies can create new problems, such as pollution from cars.
Cell phones, the internet and satellites have improved communication.
Vaccines and antibiotics have improved human health and increased life spans.
Medical technologies such as X-rays, MRI and ultrasound let doctors see inside the body.
A scientist studying the same thing in two different places should use the same methods so the results can be compared.
Scientists keep careful records so that others can repeat the investigation.
A good investigation has a large number of samples and repeated trials.
Students should record observations in a journal with the date and time.
Charts and tables organize data so it can be compared.
A scientist should change the experiment design if results cannot be measured.
Making observations over a long period of time shows patterns such as seasons and Moon phases.
Field studies observe organisms in their natural environment.
Biologists study living things; chemists study matter and chemical reactions; physicists study matter, energy and forces.
Astronomers study stars, planets and space; meteorologists study weather; geologists study rocks and Earth.
Ecologists study how organisms interact with their environment.
Botanists study plants; zoologists study animals; entomologists study insects.
Oceanographers study the ocean; paleontologists study fossils; seismologists study earthquakes.
Microbiologists study microorganisms such as bacteria.
