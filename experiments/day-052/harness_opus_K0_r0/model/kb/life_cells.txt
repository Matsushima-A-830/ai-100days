# Living things, cells, microorganisms, classification
All living things are made of one or more cells.
The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in all living things.
The cell theory states that all living things are made of cells, cells are the basic unit of life, and all cells come from existing cells.
Robert Hooke first observed and named cells when he looked at cork through a microscope.
Anton van Leeuwenhoek was the first to see living microorganisms with a microscope.
Living things need food, water, air and a place to live.
Characteristics of all living things include being made of cells, using energy, growing, developing, reproducing, responding to stimuli and getting rid of wastes.
All living things reproduce to make more of their own kind.
All living things grow and change during their lives.
All organisms respond to changes in their environment.
All organisms need energy to carry out life processes.
Nonliving things do not grow, reproduce, respond, or use energy on their own.
Rocks, water, air and sunlight are nonliving parts of an environment.
Unicellular organisms are made of only one cell, such as bacteria, amoebas and paramecia.
Multicellular organisms are made of many cells, such as plants, animals and most fungi.
In multicellular organisms, cells are specialized to do different jobs.
The levels of organization are cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism.
A tissue is a group of similar cells working together to do a job.
An organ is a group of tissues working together, such as the heart, stomach, leaf or root.
An organ system is a group of organs working together to do a job.
Muscle tissue, nerve tissue, connective tissue and epithelial tissue are types of animal tissues.
The nucleus controls the activities of the cell and contains the genetic material, DNA.
The nucleus is the control center of the cell.
The cell membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell.
The cell membrane is a thin flexible layer that surrounds the cell and protects it.
The cell wall is a rigid layer outside the cell membrane that supports and protects plant cells.
Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole; animal cells do not.
Both plant and animal cells have a nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm and mitochondria.
Plant cell walls are made of cellulose.
Cytoplasm is the jelly-like fluid inside the cell where organelles are found.
Organelles are small structures inside cells that do specific jobs.
Mitochondria are the powerhouses of the cell; they release energy from food by cellular respiration.
Mitochondria break down sugar to release energy for the cell.
Chloroplasts are organelles in plant cells that capture energy from sunlight and make food by photosynthesis.
Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, a green pigment that absorbs light energy.
Vacuoles store water, food and wastes; plant cells have a large central vacuole.
Ribosomes make proteins.
The endoplasmic reticulum transports materials within the cell.
The Golgi apparatus packages and ships proteins.
Lysosomes break down wastes and worn-out cell parts.
Chromosomes are in the nucleus and carry genes made of DNA.
Prokaryotic cells such as bacteria have no nucleus; eukaryotic cells have a nucleus.
Bacteria are single-celled organisms that have no nucleus.
Cells divide to make new cells for growth and repair.
Mitosis is cell division that produces two identical cells with the same number of chromosomes.
Meiosis is cell division that produces sex cells, eggs and sperm, with half the number of chromosomes.
Cells get the energy they need through cellular respiration.
Cellular respiration uses oxygen and glucose to release energy, producing carbon dioxide and water.
Diffusion is the movement of molecules from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration.
Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a cell membrane.
Oxygen enters cells and carbon dioxide leaves cells by diffusion.
A microscope is used to see cells and microorganisms that are too small to see with the eyes.
A compound light microscope uses lenses to magnify small objects.
Electron microscopes can magnify objects much more than light microscopes.
To find the total magnification of a microscope, multiply the eyepiece power by the objective lens power.
Microorganisms are living things too small to see without a microscope, such as bacteria, protists and some fungi.
Bacteria are found almost everywhere: in soil, water, air and inside other organisms.
Some bacteria cause diseases such as strep throat, but many bacteria are helpful.
Helpful bacteria in the intestines help digest food and make vitamins.
Bacteria are used to make foods such as yogurt and cheese.
Bacteria and fungi are decomposers that break down dead organisms and return nutrients to the soil.
Some bacteria in the roots of plants such as beans and peas change nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use.
Viruses are not cells; they can only reproduce inside a living host cell.
Viruses cause diseases such as the common cold, flu, chickenpox and measles.
Antibiotics kill bacteria but do not work against viruses.
Fungi include mushrooms, molds and yeasts.
Fungi cannot make their own food; they absorb nutrients from dead or living organisms.
Mushrooms are fungi, not plants, because they do not make their own food.
Yeast is a single-celled fungus used to make bread rise; it produces carbon dioxide gas.
Mold is a fungus that grows on old bread and fruit.
Penicillin, the first antibiotic, was discovered from a mold.
Protists are mostly single-celled organisms with a nucleus, such as amoebas, paramecia, euglena and algae.
An amoeba moves using pseudopods, or false feet.
A paramecium moves using tiny hairs called cilia.
Euglena moves using a whip-like tail called a flagellum and can make food with chloroplasts.
Algae are plantlike protists that make their own food by photosynthesis.
Algae in the ocean produce much of the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere.
Phytoplankton are tiny photosynthetic organisms that are producers in ocean food chains.
Scientists classify organisms into groups based on their similar characteristics.
Classification groups from largest to smallest are domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
A species is a group of organisms that can breed with each other and produce fertile offspring.
Scientific names use two words, the genus and species, a system created by Carl Linnaeus.
Organisms that share more classification levels are more closely related.
Organisms are classified into kingdoms: animals, plants, fungi, protists, archaea and bacteria.
Plants are multicellular, have cell walls, and make their own food by photosynthesis.
Animals are multicellular and must eat other organisms for food.
A dichotomous key is a tool that uses a series of paired choices to identify an organism.
Scientists classify animals by body structures such as backbones, body covering and how they reproduce.
Organisms that make their own food are called autotrophs or producers.
Organisms that must eat other organisms for food are called heterotrophs or consumers.
Homeostasis is the ability of an organism to keep stable conditions inside its body.
Sweating and shivering help the body keep a steady temperature, which is homeostasis.
A stimulus is something in the environment that causes an organism to react; the reaction is a response.
Plants respond to stimuli: stems grow toward light, called phototropism.
Roots grow downward in response to gravity, called gravitropism.
Some plants respond to touch, such as the Venus flytrap closing on an insect.
Organisms need water because cells are mostly water and chemical reactions in cells take place in water.
Water makes up most of the mass of a cell.
Living things are made mostly of the elements carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen.
Carbohydrates such as sugars and starches give organisms energy.
Proteins are used to build and repair body tissues.
Fats store energy.
Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions in living things.
DNA carries the instructions for the traits of an organism.
Cells use energy to grow, divide, move materials and make new substances.
Single-celled organisms carry out all life processes within one cell.
Spores are reproductive cells made by fungi, mosses and ferns.
The skin cell, nerve cell, muscle cell and blood cell have different shapes because they do different jobs.
Nerve cells are long and branched to carry messages.
Red blood cells carry oxygen and have no nucleus.
Root hair cells have a large surface area to absorb water.
Guard cells open and close stomata on leaves.
Stem cells can develop into many different kinds of cells.
Cancer is a disease in which cells divide out of control.
