# More astronomy and Earth-Moon-Sun detail
The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day.
A full moon rises at about sunset and sets at about sunrise.
A first quarter moon is high in the sky at sunset; a third quarter moon is high at sunrise.
The lunar cycle from new moon to new moon takes about 29.5 days.
A waxing crescent moon is seen in the western sky just after sunset.
After a full moon the Moon wanes through gibbous, third quarter and crescent phases.
The Moon has phases but the Sun does not, because the Sun makes its own light.
Venus and Mercury show phases like the Moon because they orbit between Earth and the Sun.
From the Moon, Earth would also appear to go through phases.
The Moon's orbit is tilted, so eclipses do not happen every month.
A total solar eclipse is seen only in a narrow path on Earth where the Moon's darkest shadow falls.
Never look directly at the Sun, even during an eclipse, because it can damage your eyes.
During a lunar eclipse the Moon can look red because sunlight bends through Earth's atmosphere.
The Moon has almost no atmosphere, so the sky on the Moon is black even in daytime.
The temperature on the Moon ranges from about 120 degrees Celsius in daytime to minus 170 degrees at night.
The Moon's surface has dust called regolith made by meteorite impacts.
The Moon has mountains, valleys, craters, maria and highlands.
Apollo astronauts brought Moon rocks back to Earth for study.
The Moon is slowly moving away from Earth, about 4 centimeters each year.
Tides rise and fall about every 12 hours and 25 minutes.
At high tide the water is high on the shore; at low tide the water is far out.
Tide pools are habitats along rocky shores that are covered at high tide and exposed at low tide.
Earth's rotation makes the Sun appear to rise in the east, reach its highest point at noon, and set in the west.
At the equator, day and night are about 12 hours each all year.
At the North Pole the Sun does not set for months in summer and does not rise for months in winter.
On the summer solstice the Sun is highest in the sky at noon and the noon shadow is shortest of the year.
On the winter solstice the Sun is lowest in the sky at noon and the noon shadow is longest of the year.
In the Northern Hemisphere the Sun rises north of east in summer and south of east in winter.
The Sun is directly overhead at the Tropic of Cancer on the June solstice and at the Tropic of Capricorn on the December solstice.
The Sun is directly over the equator on the equinoxes.
Earth's tilt points toward the same star, Polaris, all year as Earth orbits the Sun.
Sundials use shadows to tell time.
A Foucault pendulum shows that Earth rotates.
Star trails in long-exposure photographs show that Earth rotates.
Stars near Polaris circle around it and never set as seen from the Northern Hemisphere.
Seasonal constellations: Orion is seen in winter evenings and Scorpius in summer evenings in the Northern Hemisphere.
The Big Dipper is part of the constellation Ursa Major; its pointer stars point to Polaris.
Ancient sailors navigated using the stars.
Stars appear in different positions during the year because Earth orbits the Sun.
Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against distant stars as Earth orbits the Sun; it is used to measure distance.
Planets appear to wander against the background stars because they orbit the Sun.
Planets do not twinkle as much as stars because they are closer and appear as small disks.
Mars sometimes appears to move backward in the sky, called retrograde motion, as Earth passes it.
Earth is the densest planet.
Jupiter has the most mass of the planets; it is more than twice as massive as all other planets combined.
Saturn is less dense than water.
Uranus and Neptune look blue because of methane in their atmospheres.
Venus has clouds of sulfuric acid and a surface hot enough to melt lead.
Venus rotates backward compared to most planets.
Mars has seasons because its axis is tilted like Earth's.
Mars has the largest canyon in the solar system, Valles Marineris.
Dust storms on Mars can cover the whole planet.
Mars is cold because it is farther from the Sun and has a thin atmosphere.
A planet's atmosphere affects its surface temperature through the greenhouse effect.
Gravity on Mars is about one third of Earth's gravity.
Escape velocity is the speed needed to escape a planet's gravity.
Objects in orbit are falling toward the planet while moving sideways fast enough to keep missing it.
The closer a satellite is to Earth, the faster it must travel to stay in orbit.
Geostationary satellites orbit at the same rate Earth rotates, so they stay above one place.
Space debris is junk from old satellites and rockets orbiting Earth.
Astronauts exercise in space because muscles and bones weaken without gravity.
Plants grown in space need light, water and nutrients, and their roots do not know which way is down.
Telescopes on mountaintops see more clearly because there is less air and light pollution above them.
Light pollution from cities makes it hard to see stars.
Radio telescopes can work during the day and through clouds.
Spectroscopes split starlight into a spectrum to find out what elements stars are made of.
Stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
The Sun is a yellow main-sequence star of average size.
The Sun's surface temperature is about 5,500 degrees Celsius.
Betelgeuse is a red supergiant star in Orion; Rigel is a blue supergiant.
Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky.
Our galaxy contains hundreds of billions of stars.
The Sun is located in one of the spiral arms of the Milky Way, far from the center.
The center of the Milky Way contains a supermassive black hole.
The observable universe is about 93 billion light-years across.
Cosmic background radiation is evidence for the Big Bang.
