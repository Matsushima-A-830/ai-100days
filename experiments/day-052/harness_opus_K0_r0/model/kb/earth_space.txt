# Earth in space: Sun, Moon, planets, stars, day and night, seasons, tides, eclipses
The Sun is a star at the center of our solar system.
The Sun is the closest star to Earth.
The Sun is a medium-sized star made mostly of hydrogen and helium gas.
The Sun produces energy by nuclear fusion, joining hydrogen atoms to form helium.
Nuclear fusion in the core of a star releases huge amounts of energy as light and heat.
The Sun is the main source of energy for Earth.
Almost all energy on Earth comes from the Sun.
Energy from the Sun travels to Earth as light and radiation through space.
Solar energy reaches Earth by radiation, because space is a vacuum and has no matter to conduct heat.
The Sun appears larger and brighter than other stars because it is much closer to Earth.
Other stars look like tiny points of light because they are very far away.
The Sun looks bigger than other stars only because it is closer, not because it is the largest star.
Some stars are much larger and brighter than the Sun.
Sunlight takes about eight minutes to travel from the Sun to Earth.
The Sun's gravity keeps the planets in orbit around the Sun.
Gravity holds the planets in their orbits around the Sun.
Gravity keeps the Moon in orbit around Earth.
The Moon orbits Earth about once every 27 days, and the cycle of lunar phases takes about 29.5 days, about one month.
Earth rotates on its axis once every 24 hours, which causes day and night.
Day and night are caused by Earth rotating, or spinning, on its axis.
The rotation of Earth on its axis causes day and night.
Earth revolves around the Sun once every 365 days, which is one year.
One revolution of Earth around the Sun takes one year, about 365 and one fourth days.
A year is the time it takes Earth to orbit the Sun once.
A day is the time it takes Earth to rotate once on its axis.
A month is about the time it takes the Moon to go through all its phases.
Leap years add one day every four years because a year is about 365.25 days.
Earth rotates from west to east, so the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west.
The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west.
Because Earth spins from west to east, the Sun, Moon and stars appear to move across the sky from east to west.
The apparent daily motion of the Sun across the sky is caused by the rotation of Earth.
Stars appear to move across the night sky because Earth rotates.
At noon the Sun is highest in the sky, and shadows are shortest.
Shadows are longest in the early morning and late afternoon when the Sun is low in the sky.
In the morning the Sun is in the east, so shadows point west; in the afternoon shadows point east.
A shadow forms on the side of an object opposite the light source.
In the Northern Hemisphere the noon Sun is in the southern part of the sky.
The North Star, Polaris, stays almost fixed in the sky because it is nearly above Earth's North Pole.
Polaris can be used to find the direction north.
Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees.
The seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's axis as Earth revolves around the Sun.
Seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's axis, not by the distance between Earth and the Sun.
When the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun, it has summer; when it is tilted away it has winter.
When it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere, it is winter in the Southern Hemisphere.
In summer the Sun's rays strike the ground more directly and there are more hours of daylight.
In winter sunlight strikes Earth at a lower angle, spreading energy over a larger area, so it is colder.
Direct rays of sunlight heat Earth's surface more than slanted indirect rays.
Places near the equator receive the most direct sunlight and are warm all year.
The poles receive the least direct sunlight and are the coldest places on Earth.
The summer solstice, around June 21, has the most hours of daylight in the Northern Hemisphere.
The winter solstice, around December 21, has the fewest hours of daylight in the Northern Hemisphere and the longest night.
On the equinoxes in March and September, day and night are about equal in length everywhere.
The number of daylight hours changes during the year because of the tilt of Earth's axis.
Near the poles there can be 24 hours of daylight in summer and 24 hours of darkness in winter.
The Moon does not make its own light; it reflects light from the Sun.
Moonlight is sunlight reflected off the surface of the Moon.
Planets and moons are seen because they reflect sunlight.
The phases of the Moon are caused by the changing positions of the Moon, Earth and Sun as the Moon orbits Earth.
We see different phases because we see different amounts of the sunlit half of the Moon.
Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun.
The Moon phases in order are new moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, third quarter, waning crescent.
During a new moon the Moon is between Earth and the Sun and the lit side faces away from Earth.
During a full moon Earth is between the Sun and the Moon and we see the whole lit side.
Waxing means the lit part of the Moon is growing; waning means it is shrinking.
About one week after a new moon, the first quarter moon looks like a half circle.
About two weeks after a new moon there is a full moon.
The same side of the Moon always faces Earth because the Moon rotates once in the same time it takes to orbit Earth.
The Moon has no atmosphere and no liquid water.
The Moon has no air, so there is no wind or weather to erode its surface; footprints there can last a very long time.
The Moon's surface is covered with craters made by impacts of meteorites, asteroids and comets.
Craters on the Moon are made by meteorites hitting the surface.
The Moon has dark flat areas called maria made of old lava, and bright highlands.
The Moon's gravity is about one sixth of Earth's gravity, so objects weigh less on the Moon.
An astronaut's mass is the same on the Moon as on Earth, but the astronaut's weight is less on the Moon.
Weight depends on gravity, but mass stays the same everywhere.
Because the Moon has no atmosphere, temperatures on the Moon are extremely hot in sunlight and extremely cold in darkness.
Sound cannot travel on the Moon because there is no air.
The tides on Earth are caused mainly by the gravitational pull of the Moon.
The Moon's gravity pulls on Earth's oceans, causing high tides and low tides.
Most coastal places have two high tides and two low tides about every day.
Spring tides, the highest high tides, happen at new moon and full moon when the Sun, Moon and Earth are lined up.
Neap tides, with the smallest difference between high and low tide, happen at first and third quarter moons.
The Sun's gravity also affects tides, but less than the Moon because the Sun is much farther away.
A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and the Moon's shadow falls on Earth.
A solar eclipse can only happen during a new moon.
A lunar eclipse happens when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.
A lunar eclipse can only happen during a full moon.
The order of the planets from the Sun is Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.
Mercury is the planet closest to the Sun and the smallest planet.
Mercury has almost no atmosphere and has extreme temperatures.
Venus is the hottest planet because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat by the greenhouse effect.
Venus is about the same size as Earth and is very bright in the sky.
Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only planet known to have life.
Earth has liquid water, an atmosphere with oxygen, and a temperature range that supports life.
Earth's distance from the Sun lets water exist as a liquid on its surface.
Mars is the fourth planet and is called the red planet because its soil contains iron oxide, or rust.
Mars has a thin atmosphere of carbon dioxide, polar ice caps, and the largest volcano, Olympus Mons.
Mars has two small moons.
Evidence such as dry riverbeds suggests that liquid water once flowed on Mars.
Jupiter is the largest planet in the solar system.
Jupiter is a gas giant with a Great Red Spot, a giant storm.
Jupiter has many moons, including the four large Galilean moons that Galileo discovered with a telescope.
Saturn is a gas giant known for its large bright rings made of ice and rock.
Saturn has the lowest density of the planets.
Uranus is an ice giant that rotates tipped on its side.
Neptune is the farthest planet from the Sun and is a cold, windy ice giant.
Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper belt beyond Neptune.
The inner planets Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars are small, rocky terrestrial planets.
The outer planets Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune are large gas giants with rings and many moons.
The outer planets are made mostly of gases such as hydrogen and helium.
Planets farther from the Sun take longer to orbit the Sun and have longer years.
Planets farther from the Sun are colder because they receive less energy from the Sun.
Planets closer to the Sun move faster in their orbits.
The planets orbit the Sun in elliptical orbits, which are slightly flattened circles.
The asteroid belt lies between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter.
Asteroids are rocky objects that orbit the Sun, mostly in the asteroid belt.
Comets are balls of ice, dust and rock that orbit the Sun; they form glowing tails when they come near the Sun.
A comet's tail always points away from the Sun.
A meteoroid is a small piece of rock in space; a meteor is the streak of light when it burns up in the atmosphere.
A meteorite is a rock from space that reaches the surface of Earth.
Meteors are often called shooting stars, but they are not stars.
Most meteoroids burn up in Earth's atmosphere because of friction with the air.
The solar system includes the Sun, eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids and comets.
The solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a spinning cloud of gas and dust called a nebula.
Earth is about 4.6 billion years old.
Gravity pulled gas and dust together to form the Sun and planets.
A galaxy is a huge group of billions of stars held together by gravity.
Our solar system is in the Milky Way galaxy.
The Milky Way is a spiral galaxy.
Galaxies can be spiral, elliptical or irregular in shape.
The universe contains billions of galaxies.
The order from smallest to largest is planet, solar system, galaxy, universe.
A light-year is the distance light travels in one year; it is used to measure distances to stars.
After the Sun, the nearest star to Earth is Proxima Centauri, about four light-years away.
The Big Bang theory states that the universe began expanding from a very hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago.
The light from distant galaxies is shifted toward red, which shows that the universe is expanding.
Stars form in nebulas, clouds of gas and dust, when gravity pulls the material together.
The color of a star indicates its surface temperature.
Blue stars are the hottest stars, and red stars are the coolest stars.
Yellow stars like the Sun have medium surface temperatures.
The brightness of a star as seen from Earth depends on its size, its temperature, and its distance from Earth.
A star's life cycle depends on its mass.
A star like the Sun will become a red giant, then a white dwarf.
Very massive stars end in a supernova explosion and may become a neutron star or a black hole.
A black hole has gravity so strong that not even light can escape.
Stars spend most of their lives on the main sequence fusing hydrogen into helium.
A constellation is a group of stars that forms a pattern in the sky, such as the Big Dipper or Orion.
Different constellations are seen in different seasons because Earth revolves around the Sun.
Ancient people used constellations to keep track of seasons and to navigate.
Stars are visible only at night because during the day the Sun's light is so bright that it hides them.
Stars twinkle because their light passes through moving layers of Earth's atmosphere.
A telescope makes distant objects such as planets and stars appear larger and brighter.
Telescopes collect and focus light so we can see faint, distant objects in space.
Space telescopes like the Hubble orbit above Earth's atmosphere, so the atmosphere does not blur their images.
Radio telescopes detect radio waves from objects in space.
A satellite is an object that orbits a planet; the Moon is Earth's natural satellite.
Artificial satellites orbit Earth and are used for communication, weather forecasting, GPS navigation and studying Earth.
Weather satellites take pictures of clouds and storms from space.
Rockets must reach high speed to overcome Earth's gravity.
Astronauts in orbit float because they are in free fall around Earth.
Space probes and rovers have explored other planets such as Mars.
The International Space Station orbits Earth and astronauts live and do experiments there.
Spacesuits protect astronauts from the lack of air, extreme temperatures and radiation in space.
Copernicus proposed that the planets, including Earth, revolve around the Sun, a sun-centered or heliocentric model.
Early people believed in a geocentric model in which the Sun and planets moved around Earth.
Galileo used a telescope to observe the moons of Jupiter and the phases of Venus, supporting the heliocentric model.
Kepler discovered that planets move in elliptical orbits.
Newton explained that gravity keeps planets in orbit around the Sun.
The force of gravity between two objects depends on their masses and the distance between them.
Gravity is stronger between objects with more mass and weaker when they are farther apart.
Larger planets with more mass have stronger surface gravity.
Jupiter has the strongest gravity of the planets because it has the most mass.
Earth's gravity pulls objects toward the center of Earth.
Gravity is the force that causes objects to fall to the ground.
Gravity is the force that pulls rain and snow down to Earth's surface.
The Sun is about 150 million kilometers, or 93 million miles, from Earth.
The Sun's diameter is about 109 times Earth's diameter.
The Moon is about 384,000 kilometers from Earth.
The Moon is much smaller than Earth, about one fourth of Earth's diameter.
Sunspots are cooler, darker areas on the Sun's surface caused by magnetic activity.
Solar flares release bursts of energy and charged particles from the Sun.
Solar wind is a stream of charged particles from the Sun.
The aurora, or northern lights, happens when charged particles from the Sun hit gases in Earth's upper atmosphere near the poles.
Earth's magnetic field protects Earth from the solar wind.
Earth's atmosphere protects the surface from most meteoroids and harmful ultraviolet radiation.
Earth is the only planet in our solar system with large amounts of liquid water on its surface.
A model of the solar system can show the relative sizes of the planets and their distances from the Sun.
Time zones exist because Earth rotates and different places face the Sun at different times.
As Earth rotates, places on Earth move from daylight into darkness.
The side of Earth facing the Sun has day; the side facing away from the Sun has night.
If Earth did not rotate, one side would always have day and the other side would always have night.
If Earth's axis were not tilted, there would be no seasons.
Earth is slightly closer to the Sun in January than in July, which shows distance does not cause seasons.
The length of a day on a planet depends on how fast the planet rotates on its axis.
The length of a year on a planet depends on how long it takes the planet to revolve around the Sun.
Venus rotates very slowly, so a day on Venus is longer than its year.
Gas giants have no solid surface.
The Sun's core temperature is millions of degrees.
The Sun will continue to shine for about five billion more years.
Earth's Moon probably formed from debris after a large object collided with the young Earth.
