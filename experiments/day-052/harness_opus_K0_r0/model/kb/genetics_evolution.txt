# Heredity, genetics, evolution, natural selection
Heredity is the passing of traits from parents to offspring.
Inherited traits are passed from parents to offspring through genes.
Genes are sections of DNA on chromosomes that carry instructions for traits.
DNA is the molecule in cells that carries genetic information; it is shaped like a double helix.
Offspring get half of their genes from the mother and half from the father.
Each parent passes one allele of each gene to its offspring.
Inherited traits include eye color, hair color, skin color, height potential, blood type, freckles, dimples and attached earlobes.
In plants, inherited traits include flower color, seed shape and leaf shape.
Acquired traits are characteristics an organism gets during its life, such as scars, learned skills, or muscles from exercise; they are not inherited.
Learned behaviors, like reading or riding a bike, are not inherited.
A scar, a haircut or a tan are acquired traits that cannot be passed to offspring.
Some traits are affected by both genes and the environment, such as height and weight.
Gregor Mendel is called the father of genetics; he studied inheritance in pea plants.
Mendel found that traits are controlled by factors, now called genes, that come in pairs.
A dominant allele hides the effect of a recessive allele.
A recessive trait appears only when an organism inherits two recessive alleles.
Dominant alleles are written with a capital letter, such as T, and recessive alleles with a lowercase letter, such as t.
Genotype is the genetic makeup of an organism, such as Tt; phenotype is its physical appearance, such as tall.
Homozygous means having two identical alleles, such as TT or tt; heterozygous means two different alleles, such as Tt.
A Punnett square is a chart used to predict the possible genotypes of offspring.
If both parents are heterozygous Tt, about 3 out of 4 offspring show the dominant trait and 1 out of 4 shows the recessive trait.
A cross between TT and tt produces all Tt offspring, which all show the dominant trait.
A cross between Tt and tt produces about half dominant and half recessive offspring.
Two parents with a dominant trait can have a child with a recessive trait if both carry the recessive allele.
Sexual reproduction combines genetic material from two parents, so offspring are different from each parent.
Sexual reproduction produces genetic variation among offspring.
Asexual reproduction involves only one parent, and offspring are genetically identical to the parent.
Examples of asexual reproduction are budding in yeast and hydra, binary fission in bacteria, and plants growing from runners or cuttings.
Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission, splitting into two cells.
Clones are genetically identical copies of an organism.
Sex cells, eggs and sperm, have half the number of chromosomes of body cells.
Humans have 46 chromosomes, or 23 pairs, in each body cell.
Sex cells in humans have 23 chromosomes.
Fertilization joins an egg and sperm, restoring the full number of chromosomes.
A mutation is a change in the DNA of a gene.
Mutations can be harmful, helpful or have no effect.
Mutations in sex cells can be passed to offspring.
Mutations can be caused by radiation, such as X-rays and ultraviolet light, and by some chemicals.
Genetic disorders such as sickle cell anemia, cystic fibrosis and hemophilia are caused by mutated genes.
Genetic engineering changes the DNA of an organism, for example to make bacteria produce insulin.
Selective breeding, or artificial selection, is when people choose organisms with desired traits to breed.
Farmers use selective breeding to produce crops and livestock with desirable traits, such as cows that give more milk.
Different breeds of dogs were produced by selective breeding.
Evolution is the change in the inherited traits of a species over many generations.
Charles Darwin developed the theory of evolution by natural selection after studying finches and tortoises on the Galapagos Islands.
Darwin observed that finches on different Galapagos islands had different beak shapes suited to the food on each island.
Natural selection is the process in which organisms with traits best suited to their environment survive and reproduce more.
In natural selection, individuals with helpful variations survive and pass those traits to their offspring.
Natural selection requires variation in traits, inheritance, overproduction of offspring and competition for resources.
Organisms produce more offspring than can survive, so they compete for food, water and space.
Over many generations, helpful traits become more common in a population.
Variation means differences in traits among individuals of the same species.
Variations come from mutations and from sexual reproduction.
Peppered moths: when trees became dark from pollution, dark moths were better camouflaged and became more common.
Bacteria can evolve resistance to antibiotics through natural selection when antibiotics are overused.
Insects can become resistant to pesticides over many generations.
Species that cannot adapt to changes in their environment may become extinct.
Extinction happens when the last member of a species dies.
Causes of extinction include habitat loss, climate change, new predators or diseases, overhunting and competition.
Fossils provide evidence of evolution by showing how organisms changed over time.
Similar bone structures, called homologous structures, in different animals such as a human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper show common ancestry.
Similarities in DNA and embryos show that organisms are related.
Organisms with more similar DNA are more closely related.
Over long periods, isolated populations can evolve into new species.
Geographic isolation, such as by a mountain range or an ocean, can lead to new species.
Adaptations develop in a population over many generations, not in one individual's lifetime.
An individual organism cannot change its genes to adapt; populations change over generations.
The giraffe's long neck evolved because giraffes with longer necks could reach more food and had more offspring.
Lamarck wrongly proposed that organisms pass on traits acquired during their lifetime.
Biodiversity is the variety of living things in an area.
Greater genetic diversity helps a population survive changes in the environment and disease.
Populations with little genetic variation are more likely to be wiped out by disease or environmental change.
Identical twins develop from one fertilized egg and have the same DNA.
Fraternal twins develop from two different eggs and are no more alike than other siblings.
Traits controlled by many genes, such as human height and skin color, show a wide range of variation.
Chromosomes come in pairs, one from each parent.
The sex of a human baby is determined by the father's sperm, which carries an X or a Y chromosome.
Females have two X chromosomes; males have an X and a Y chromosome.
Offspring of the same parents can look different because each inherits a different combination of genes.
Cloned plants from cuttings have the same traits as the parent plant.
Pollen from one plant fertilizing another plant is cross-pollination and produces variation.
DNA is copied before a cell divides so each new cell gets a complete set.
The structure of DNA was discovered by Watson and Crick with help from Rosalind Franklin's X-ray images.
Genes determine the proteins a cell makes, and proteins determine traits.
A pedigree is a chart that shows how a trait is inherited in a family over generations.
The environment can affect how genes are expressed, such as hydrangea flower color depending on soil acidity.
Flamingos are pink because of the food they eat, an environmental effect.
