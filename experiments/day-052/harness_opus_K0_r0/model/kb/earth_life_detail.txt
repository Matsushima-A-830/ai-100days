# More Earth science and life science detail
Earth's spheres are the geosphere (rock), hydrosphere (water), atmosphere (air) and biosphere (living things).
The hydrosphere includes all the water on Earth: oceans, lakes, rivers, groundwater, ice and water vapor.
The geosphere, or lithosphere, is the solid rocky part of Earth.
The ocean floor has features such as the continental shelf, continental slope, abyssal plain, mid-ocean ridges and trenches.
The Mariana Trench is the deepest part of the ocean.
The continental shelf is the shallow part of the ocean floor next to the continents.
Mid-ocean ridges are underwater mountain ranges where plates spread apart.
Salinity is the amount of salt in water; ocean water has a salinity of about 3.5 percent.
Ocean water becomes colder and pressure increases with depth.
Sunlight only reaches the top part of the ocean, called the sunlit zone.
Upwelling brings cold, nutrient-rich water from the deep ocean to the surface, supporting fishing.
Rivers flow downhill from high places to lower places and eventually to the ocean.
A river's source is where it begins; its mouth is where it ends.
Meanders are curves in a slow-moving river.
Floodplains are flat areas next to rivers that are covered with water during floods and have fertile soil.
Oxbow lakes form when a meander is cut off from a river.
Springs are places where groundwater flows out at the surface.
Lakes form in low areas where water collects, such as basins carved by glaciers.
The water table is the top of the zone where the ground is filled with water.
Porous rocks such as sandstone have many spaces that hold water.
Soil horizons are layers of soil: topsoil (A), subsoil (B) and weathered rock (C) above bedrock.
Bedrock is the solid rock beneath the soil.
Soil in the tropical rain forest is thin and poor because heavy rains wash away nutrients.
Prairie soils are deep and rich, making grasslands good for farming.
Permafrost is permanently frozen ground in the tundra.
Air masses are named for where they form: maritime air is moist, continental air is dry, tropical air is warm and polar air is cold.
A maritime tropical air mass forms over warm ocean water and is warm and humid.
A continental polar air mass forms over cold land and is cold and dry.
Isobars on a weather map connect places with the same air pressure.
Closely spaced isobars mean strong winds.
An H on a weather map marks a high-pressure area; an L marks a low-pressure area.
In the Northern Hemisphere, winds blow clockwise around high pressure and counterclockwise around low pressure.
Weather instruments are placed in a shaded, open area so they give accurate readings.
Relative humidity of 100 percent means the air is saturated and fog or rain may form.
A psychrometer uses a wet bulb and a dry bulb thermometer to measure relative humidity.
Clouds and precipitation are more likely when humidity is high and air pressure is falling.
Monsoons are seasonal winds that bring heavy rain to places such as India.
Lake-effect snow forms when cold air moves over warm lake water, picking up moisture.
Earth's average surface temperature is about 15 degrees Celsius.
Latitude is the main factor in determining climate.
The tropics lie between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn.
The Arctic Circle and Antarctic Circle mark where there is at least one day of 24-hour daylight or darkness.
Antarctica is the coldest, windiest and driest continent; it is covered by an ice sheet.
The Sahara is the largest hot desert.
The Amazon rain forest is the largest tropical rain forest.
The Sun's layers include the core, radiative zone, convection zone, photosphere, chromosphere and corona.
The photosphere is the visible surface of the Sun.
The corona is the Sun's outer atmosphere, seen during a total solar eclipse.
Stars are classified by color, temperature, size and brightness on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram.
Apparent magnitude is how bright a star looks from Earth; absolute magnitude is its true brightness.
Most stars are main-sequence stars like the Sun.
Red giants and supergiants are very large, cool stars near the end of their lives.
White dwarfs are small, hot, dim remains of stars like the Sun.
A star's mass determines how long it lives; massive stars burn out faster.
Our Sun is about halfway through its life.
Light from distant stars takes many years to reach Earth, so we see stars as they were in the past.
The Andromeda galaxy is the nearest large spiral galaxy to the Milky Way.
The Sun takes about 225 million years to orbit the center of the Milky Way.
Earth's orbit around the Sun is nearly circular.
Earth travels around the Sun at about 30 kilometers per second.
A day on Earth is 24 hours; a day on Jupiter is about 10 hours, the shortest of the planets.
Mercury has the shortest year, 88 Earth days; Neptune has the longest year, about 165 Earth years.
Neptune has the longest year of all the planets because it is farthest from the Sun.
Mercury has the shortest year because it is closest to the Sun.
Saturn's rings are made of pieces of ice and rock.
Titan is Saturn's largest moon and has a thick atmosphere.
Europa, a moon of Jupiter, may have liquid water under its icy surface.
Io, a moon of Jupiter, has active volcanoes.
Ganymede, a moon of Jupiter, is the largest moon in the solar system.
The Kuiper belt is a region of icy objects beyond Neptune.
Halley's comet returns about every 76 years.
Meteor showers happen when Earth passes through the dust left by a comet.
Cells of the human body include skin cells, muscle cells, nerve cells, bone cells, blood cells and fat cells.
Unicellular organisms take in food, release wastes and reproduce within a single cell.
Plant cells are usually rectangular because of their cell walls; animal cells are rounder.
Cell size is limited because a cell needs enough surface area of membrane to take in nutrients and remove wastes.
Active transport uses energy to move substances across a cell membrane.
The cell membrane is selectively permeable; it lets some substances pass and blocks others.
Hormones in plants control growth, such as growth toward light.
Plants have no nervous system but respond to light, gravity, water and touch.
Green plants are found at the start of food chains because they convert light energy to chemical energy.
The leaves of a plant are its food factories.
Animals that eat plants get energy that the plants stored from sunlight.
Plants need nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium from the soil.
Nitrogen is needed by plants to make proteins; most plants cannot use nitrogen gas directly from the air.
Lightning and bacteria change nitrogen in the air into compounds plants can use.
Seed plants include flowering plants and conifers; seedless plants include mosses and ferns.
Plants that live in water, like seaweed, do not need strong stems because water supports them.
Trees provide shade, food, oxygen, habitat and wood.
Old-growth forests have trees hundreds of years old and many species.
Coral reefs are threatened by warming oceans, pollution and coral bleaching.
Kelp forests are underwater forests of large brown algae that provide habitat for many sea animals.
Sea otters eat sea urchins, which protects kelp forests.
Wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park and changed the ecosystem by controlling elk populations.
Salmon carry nutrients from the ocean to forests when bears eat them.
Animals adapted to grasslands are often fast runners or burrowers.
Rain forest animals often live in trees and may be brightly colored.
Ocean animals living deep in the dark have adaptations such as bioluminescence and large eyes.
Animals in the tundra have thick fur, small ears and short legs to conserve heat.
Animals that live in hot deserts often get water from their food.
Kangaroo rats can survive without drinking water by getting water from seeds.
Earthworms, insects and other small animals help aerate soil.
Owls cough up pellets containing the bones and fur of the animals they eat; scientists study pellets to learn what owls eat.
Scientists use tagging and tracking collars to study animal migration.
Bird beaks, feet and wings are adapted for the way each bird lives and eats.
Eagles and hawks have sharp talons for grasping prey.
Ducks have webbed feet for swimming; woodpeckers have feet for clinging to trees; ostriches have strong feet for running.
Penguins have flippers instead of wings for flying and a thick layer of fat to stay warm.
Flamingos filter food from water with their bent beaks.
The anteater has a long sticky tongue for catching ants.
Giraffes, horses and deer have eyes on the sides of their heads to watch for predators.
Bats, owls and cats can find prey in the dark using hearing, large eyes or echolocation.
Snakes sense heat from warm-blooded prey with special pits.
Sharks can detect the electric fields of prey and smell blood from far away.
Whales have blubber and blowholes for breathing at the surface.
Fish gills absorb dissolved oxygen from water.
Tadpoles breathe with gills; adult frogs breathe with lungs and through their skin.
Insects breathe through tiny holes called spiracles.
The human body's response to a hot day is to sweat; the response to cold is to shiver.
A healthy human heart beats about 60 to 100 times per minute at rest.
Breathing rate and heart rate return to normal after exercise.
Exercising muscles produce heat and carbon dioxide and use oxygen and glucose.
Lactic acid builds up in muscles during hard exercise and can cause soreness.
Bones are living tissue that can repair themselves when broken.
Babies have more bones than adults because some bones fuse together as they grow.
Hair and nails are made of a protein called keratin.
Fingerprints are unique to each person.
Taste buds on the tongue detect sweet, sour, salty, bitter and savory tastes.
The sense of smell is closely linked to the sense of taste.
Nerves in the skin detect heat, cold, pressure and pain.
Pain is a signal that warns the body of injury.
