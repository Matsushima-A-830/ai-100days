# Forces, motion, simple machines, energy forms and transformations, heat transfer
A force is a push or a pull.
Forces can change the speed, direction or shape of an object.
Force is measured in newtons using a spring scale.
Gravity is a force that pulls objects toward each other; on Earth it pulls objects toward the ground.
Friction is a force that opposes motion between two surfaces that touch.
Friction slows down moving objects and produces heat.
Rubbing your hands together makes them warm because of friction.
Rough surfaces produce more friction than smooth surfaces.
Oil, grease, wax and other lubricants reduce friction.
Ice is slippery because it has very little friction.
Friction between tires and the road helps cars stop and turn; sand or salt on icy roads increases friction.
Air resistance is friction from air that slows falling and moving objects.
A parachute increases air resistance to slow a fall.
Water resistance, or drag, slows objects moving through water.
Magnetism is a force that can act at a distance.
Gravity, magnetism and electric force can act without touching.
Balanced forces are equal in size and opposite in direction; they do not change an object's motion.
Unbalanced forces cause an object to start moving, stop, speed up, slow down or change direction.
The net force is the total of all forces acting on an object.
Newton's first law, the law of inertia, says an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion unless acted on by an unbalanced force.
Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its motion; objects with more mass have more inertia.
Seat belts protect passengers because their bodies keep moving forward due to inertia when a car stops suddenly.
Newton's second law says force equals mass times acceleration.
More force is needed to accelerate an object with more mass.
The same force gives a smaller acceleration to a heavier object.
Newton's third law says for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.
A rocket moves forward because hot gases push backward out of the engine; the gases push the rocket forward.
When you swim, you push water backward and the water pushes you forward.
When a person jumps off a boat, the boat moves in the opposite direction.
Motion is a change in position over time.
Speed is the distance traveled divided by the time it takes; speed equals distance divided by time.
Average speed equals total distance divided by total time.
Speed is measured in units such as meters per second, kilometers per hour or miles per hour.
Velocity is speed in a certain direction.
Acceleration is a change in velocity, including speeding up, slowing down or changing direction.
An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating because its direction keeps changing.
A distance-time graph with a steeper line shows a faster speed; a flat line shows the object is not moving.
To compare speeds, objects must be timed over the same distance.
Momentum is mass times velocity.
All objects fall at the same rate in a vacuum, regardless of mass.
Without air resistance, a feather and a hammer fall at the same rate, as shown on the Moon.
A falling object speeds up as it falls because of gravity.
Objects thrown upward slow down, stop and fall back because gravity pulls them down.
Weight is the force of gravity on an object; weight depends on mass and gravity.
An object's weight would change on another planet, but its mass would stay the same.
Buoyancy is the upward force of a fluid on an object; objects float when buoyancy equals their weight.
Ships made of steel float because their shape displaces a large amount of water.
Pressure is force divided by area.
Sharp knives cut easily because the force is concentrated on a small area.
Snowshoes spread weight over a larger area so a person does not sink into snow.
Work is done when a force moves an object a distance; work equals force times distance.
No work is done if the object does not move.
Power is the rate of doing work, work divided by time, measured in watts.
Simple machines make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force.
The six simple machines are the lever, pulley, wheel and axle, inclined plane, wedge and screw.
Simple machines do not reduce the amount of work; they let you use less force over a greater distance.
A lever is a bar that turns on a fixed point called a fulcrum.
A seesaw, crowbar, bottle opener, scissors, wheelbarrow, hammer pulling a nail and a broom are levers.
Moving the fulcrum closer to the load makes the load easier to lift with a lever.
A pulley is a wheel with a rope or chain around it, used to lift loads; a flagpole uses a pulley to raise a flag.
A single fixed pulley changes the direction of the force, so you pull down to lift a load up.
A movable pulley or a system of pulleys reduces the force needed to lift a heavy load.
Cranes and elevators use pulleys to lift heavy objects.
An inclined plane, or ramp, is a flat sloping surface that makes it easier to move objects up to a higher place.
A longer, less steep ramp requires less force but a longer distance.
A wedge is two inclined planes together used to split or cut things, like an ax, a knife or a doorstop.
A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a post, used to hold things together or lift.
A wheel and axle is a large wheel attached to a smaller rod; doorknobs, steering wheels and screwdrivers are wheels and axles.
Gears are wheels with teeth that transfer motion and force, as in bicycles and clocks.
A compound machine is made of two or more simple machines, such as scissors, a bicycle or a can opener.
Mechanical advantage tells how many times a machine multiplies the input force.
Energy is the ability to do work or cause change.
The law of conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed; it only changes from one form to another.
Kinetic energy is the energy of motion; faster and heavier objects have more kinetic energy.
Potential energy is stored energy due to position or condition.
Gravitational potential energy depends on an object's height and mass; higher and heavier objects have more.
A stretched rubber band, a compressed spring and a drawn bow store elastic potential energy.
Water stored behind a dam has potential energy.
A rock at the top of a hill has potential energy, which changes to kinetic energy as it rolls down.
As a ball falls, potential energy changes into kinetic energy.
A roller coaster has the most potential energy at the top of the highest hill and the most kinetic energy at the bottom.
A swinging pendulum changes potential energy to kinetic energy and back again.
Mechanical energy is the total kinetic and potential energy of an object.
Forms of energy include mechanical, thermal or heat, chemical, electrical, light or radiant, sound, magnetic and nuclear energy.
Chemical energy is stored in the bonds of molecules, in food, fuel, wood and batteries.
Food contains chemical energy that the body uses.
Batteries store chemical energy and change it into electrical energy.
In a flashlight, chemical energy in the battery changes to electrical energy, then to light and heat.
A light bulb changes electrical energy into light energy and heat.
A toaster changes electrical energy into heat, or thermal energy.
A fan or motor changes electrical energy into mechanical energy, motion.
A radio or speaker changes electrical energy into sound energy.
A generator changes mechanical energy into electrical energy.
Solar panels, or solar cells, change light energy from the Sun into electrical energy.
Plants change light energy into chemical energy during photosynthesis.
A car engine changes chemical energy in gasoline into mechanical energy and heat.
Burning wood changes chemical energy into heat and light.
Wind turbines change the kinetic energy of wind into electrical energy.
Hydroelectric dams change the energy of falling or moving water into electrical energy.
When you eat food, chemical energy changes into the mechanical energy of your muscles and heat.
Muscles change chemical energy into mechanical energy.
Nuclear energy is stored in the nucleus of atoms and released by fission or fusion.
Nuclear power plants split uranium atoms, called fission, to produce heat that makes electricity.
In every energy change some energy is converted to heat, which is often wasted.
Energy-efficient devices waste less energy as heat.
LED and fluorescent bulbs are more efficient than incandescent bulbs because they give off less heat.
Thermal energy is the total energy of motion of the particles in a substance.
Heat is the transfer of thermal energy from a warmer object to a cooler object.
Heat always flows from hot objects to cold objects until they reach the same temperature.
When you hold an ice cube, heat moves from your hand to the ice.
A cold drink warms up because heat flows from the warmer air into the drink.
Heat moves in three ways: conduction, convection and radiation.
Conduction is the transfer of heat through direct contact, such as a metal spoon getting hot in hot soup.
A metal pan on a stove heats by conduction.
Convection is the transfer of heat by the movement of a fluid, a liquid or gas, as warm fluid rises and cool fluid sinks.
Convection currents form in boiling water, in the air of a room, and in Earth's mantle.
Radiation is the transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves, which can travel through empty space.
The Sun heats Earth by radiation.
Feeling the warmth of a campfire from a distance is heat transfer by radiation.
Conductors let heat or electricity pass through easily; metals are good conductors.
Insulators slow the transfer of heat or electricity; wood, plastic, rubber, cloth, foam, glass and air are insulators.
Pot handles are made of plastic or wood because they are insulators and do not get hot.
A thermos or insulated cup keeps hot drinks hot and cold drinks cold by reducing heat transfer.
Wool coats and blankets keep you warm by trapping air, an insulator, that slows heat loss.
Houses are insulated with fiberglass or foam in walls and attics to reduce heat loss and save energy.
Double-pane windows with air between the glass reduce heat transfer.
Dark colors absorb more light and heat than light colors; white and shiny surfaces reflect light and heat.
A black object in sunlight gets warmer than a white object because it absorbs more light energy.
Wearing light-colored clothing in summer keeps you cooler because it reflects sunlight.
Temperature is measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit.
Thermal energy depends on temperature and mass; a bathtub of warm water has more thermal energy than a cup of hot water.
Metal feels colder than wood at the same temperature because metal conducts heat away from your hand faster.
Energy from fuels is released as heat when they burn.
Sound energy is produced by vibrating objects.
Light energy travels as waves and can travel through space.
Electrical energy is the energy of moving electric charges.
A moving object can transfer energy when it hits another object, as when a bowling ball knocks down pins.
The higher a ball is dropped from, the higher it bounces, because it had more potential energy.
A ball bounces lower each time because some energy changes to heat and sound.
Energy is transferred when objects collide.
A spring scale measures force or weight in newtons.
Pushing a heavy box takes more force than pushing a light box.
A car traveling faster takes a longer distance to stop.
Wheels and ball bearings reduce friction.
Streamlined shapes reduce air resistance on cars, planes and fast animals.
Brakes use friction to slow down wheels.
Walking requires friction between shoes and the ground.
Shoes with treads have more friction and help prevent slipping.
A magnet can push or pull without touching an object.
A rolling ball eventually stops because of friction.
In space with no friction or air resistance, a moving object would keep moving in a straight line.
Gravity causes a ball rolled off a table to fall to the floor in a curved path.
Increasing the angle of a ramp makes a ball roll down faster.
A heavier object needs more force to start moving and to stop.
