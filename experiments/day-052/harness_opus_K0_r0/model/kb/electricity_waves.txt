# Electricity, magnetism, sound, light, waves
Electricity is the flow of electric charges, usually electrons.
Electric current is the flow of electrons through a conductor such as a wire.
A circuit is a closed path through which electric current flows.
A complete, or closed, circuit is needed for electric current to flow and for a bulb to light.
An open circuit has a break or gap, so current cannot flow and the bulb does not light.
A simple circuit has a power source such as a battery, wires, and a load such as a light bulb.
A switch opens and closes a circuit to turn a device on and off.
A battery is a source of electrical energy in a circuit.
In a series circuit, there is only one path for the current; if one bulb goes out, all the bulbs go out.
In a parallel circuit, there is more than one path; if one bulb goes out, the others stay lit.
Homes are wired with parallel circuits so each device can be turned on and off separately.
Adding more batteries in series makes a bulb brighter.
Electrical conductors such as copper, silver, aluminum, gold and other metals let electricity flow easily.
Electrical insulators such as rubber, plastic, glass, wood and cloth do not let electricity flow easily.
Wires are made of copper covered with plastic or rubber insulation for safety.
Water, especially salt water, conducts electricity, so electrical devices should be kept away from water.
Resistance is the opposition to the flow of electric current; it is measured in ohms.
Voltage is the push that moves current through a circuit; it is measured in volts.
Current is measured in amperes, or amps.
Ohm's law: voltage equals current times resistance.
Electric power is measured in watts; energy used is measured in kilowatt-hours.
A fuse or circuit breaker stops current if too much flows, preventing fires.
Short circuits can cause wires to overheat and start fires.
Static electricity is the buildup of electric charge on the surface of an object.
Rubbing a balloon on hair transfers electrons and gives the balloon a static charge, so it sticks to a wall.
Objects with opposite charges attract; objects with like or the same charges repel.
A positive charge and a negative charge attract each other.
Two negative charges repel each other.
A static shock happens when built-up charges jump from one object to another, as after walking across a carpet.
Lightning is a huge discharge of static electricity.
A magnet is an object that attracts iron, nickel and cobalt.
Magnets attract objects made of iron or steel, such as nails, paper clips and pins.
Magnets do not attract aluminum, copper, gold, silver, plastic, glass, wood, paper or rubber.
Aluminum cans are not attracted to magnets, but steel cans are.
Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole.
Like magnetic poles repel each other; opposite poles attract.
A north pole and a south pole attract; two north poles repel.
The magnetic force is strongest at the poles of a magnet.
A magnetic field is the area around a magnet where its force acts.
Iron filings sprinkled around a magnet show the pattern of its magnetic field.
If a magnet is broken in half, each piece has a north pole and a south pole.
Earth acts like a giant magnet with a magnetic field.
A compass needle is a small magnet that lines up with Earth's magnetic field and points north.
Compasses are used for navigation to find direction.
An electromagnet is made by wrapping a coiled wire around an iron core and passing electric current through it.
An electromagnet can be turned on and off, and it becomes stronger with more coils of wire or more current.
Electromagnets are used in cranes that lift scrap metal, in motors, doorbells, speakers and MRI machines.
Electric current produces a magnetic field.
Moving a magnet inside a coil of wire produces an electric current; this is how generators work.
Electric motors use magnets and electricity to produce motion.
Generators in power plants turn turbines to produce electricity.
Most electricity is produced in power plants by spinning turbines connected to generators.
Electricity travels from power plants to homes through power lines.
A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from one place to another without carrying matter.
Waves transfer energy, not matter.
Mechanical waves, such as sound waves, water waves and seismic waves, need a medium to travel through.
A medium is the matter, solid, liquid or gas, that a wave travels through.
Electromagnetic waves, such as light, can travel through empty space, a vacuum.
In a transverse wave, the particles of the medium move perpendicular, or at right angles, to the direction the wave travels.
Light waves and waves on a rope are transverse waves.
In a longitudinal or compression wave, the particles of the medium move back and forth parallel to the direction of the wave.
Sound waves are longitudinal waves made of compressions and rarefactions.
The crest is the highest point of a wave and the trough is the lowest point.
Wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next crest.
Amplitude is the height of a wave; it is related to the energy of the wave.
Frequency is the number of waves that pass a point each second, measured in hertz.
Higher frequency waves have shorter wavelengths.
Waves with larger amplitude carry more energy.
Sound is produced by vibrations.
Sound is a form of energy caused by objects vibrating, which makes the air vibrate.
When a guitar string is plucked, it vibrates and produces sound.
Sound needs a medium to travel; it cannot travel through empty space, a vacuum.
There is no sound in space because there is no air to carry sound waves.
Sound travels fastest through solids, slower through liquids, and slowest through gases.
Sound travels faster in water than in air, and faster in steel than in water.
The speed of sound in air is about 343 meters per second.
Light travels much faster than sound, which is why we see lightning before we hear thunder.
Pitch is how high or low a sound is; it depends on the frequency of the vibrations.
Faster vibrations make a higher pitch; slower vibrations make a lower pitch.
Shorter, thinner or tighter strings vibrate faster and make a higher pitch.
Longer, thicker or looser strings make a lower pitch.
A short column of air in a flute or a bottle with more water makes a higher pitch.
Volume, or loudness, depends on the amplitude of sound waves; more energy makes a louder sound.
Loudness is measured in decibels.
Hitting a drum harder makes a louder sound because the vibrations have greater amplitude.
An echo is a sound wave that reflects off a surface and returns.
Soft materials like curtains, carpets and foam absorb sound and reduce echoes.
Sonar uses reflected sound waves to find the depth of the ocean and locate objects underwater.
Ultrasound uses high-frequency sound waves to make images inside the body.
Humans hear sounds from about 20 to 20,000 hertz; dogs and bats can hear higher pitches.
Loud sounds can damage hearing; ear protection should be worn around loud machines.
Light is a form of energy that travels in straight lines as electromagnetic waves.
Light travels at about 300,000 kilometers per second, the fastest speed in the universe.
Light can travel through a vacuum, so sunlight reaches Earth through space.
Luminous objects such as the Sun, stars, fire and light bulbs give off their own light.
Most objects are seen because they reflect light into our eyes.
We see an object when light reflects off it and enters our eyes.
Reflection is the bouncing of light off a surface.
A mirror reflects light; smooth shiny surfaces reflect light best.
The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
A plane mirror makes an image that is reversed left to right.
Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one medium to another, such as from air into water or glass.
A straw in a glass of water looks bent because of refraction.
Lenses refract light to form images.
A convex lens is thicker in the middle and bends light rays together to focus them; magnifying glasses use convex lenses.
A concave lens is thinner in the middle and spreads light rays apart.
Eyeglasses use lenses to correct vision by refracting light.
Microscopes, telescopes, cameras and binoculars use lenses.
Absorption happens when a material takes in light and changes it to heat.
Transparent materials such as clear glass, water and air let almost all light pass through.
Translucent materials such as wax paper, frosted glass and thin cloth let some light through but scatter it.
Opaque materials such as wood, metal, rock and cardboard block light and make shadows.
A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light.
A shadow is larger when the object is closer to the light source.
White light, such as sunlight, is made of all the colors of the rainbow.
A prism separates white light into the colors of the spectrum: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.
Red light has the longest wavelength of visible light and violet light has the shortest.
An object's color is the color of light it reflects; other colors are absorbed.
A red apple looks red because it reflects red light and absorbs other colors.
A white object reflects all colors of light; a black object absorbs all colors of light.
Black objects get hotter in sunlight because they absorb all the light.
The electromagnetic spectrum includes radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.
Radio waves have the longest wavelengths and the lowest energy; gamma rays have the shortest wavelengths and the highest energy.
Infrared radiation is felt as heat.
Ultraviolet light from the Sun causes sunburn and can damage skin.
X-rays pass through soft tissue but are blocked by bones, so they are used to make images of bones.
Microwaves are used to cook food and for communication.
Radio waves are used for radio, television and cell phone communication.
Lasers produce a narrow, intense beam of light of one color.
Fiber optic cables carry light signals for communication.
Plants absorb red and blue light and reflect green light.
Animals that are active at night have large eyes to gather more light.
The pupil of the eye lets light in, and the lens focuses it on the retina.
Light from the Sun is needed for plants to grow and for us to see.
Sunlight travels to Earth in about eight minutes.
Solar cells convert light energy into electrical energy.
A periscope uses mirrors to see over objects.
Telescopes use lenses or mirrors to collect light from distant objects.
Light travels in a straight line until it hits an object.
A flashlight beam shows that light travels in straight lines.
Energy from waves can be harmful: loud sound damages ears, ultraviolet light burns skin, X-rays and gamma rays damage cells.
Ocean waves are caused mostly by wind and carry energy to the shore.
A seismic wave carries energy from an earthquake through Earth.
Particles of a medium move back and forth or up and down as a wave passes, but they do not travel along with the wave.
A cork on water bobs up and down as a wave passes but does not move along with the wave.
Telephones change sound into electrical signals, and speakers change electrical signals back into sound.
A microphone changes sound energy into electrical energy.
Digital signals are more reliable than analog signals and can be stored and copied without losing quality.
